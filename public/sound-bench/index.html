The Cabinet of Curios

Sound Bench

Most sound effects in a browser game are built from just two ingredients: tones and noise. Work through the stations to hear what each piece does to a sound, then open the bench and take apart every sound in Hardpoint and Cozy Submarine, or build your own.

Every station has a Play button. Change a control, press Play again, and listen for the difference. The pictures are drawn from the exact sound you'll hear. No sound on a phone? Check its volume.

Station 1

A tone

A tone is one shape of wave repeated over and over. How many times it repeats each second is its pitch, counted in hertz (Hz). The shape sets its color: a sine is pure and soft, a triangle a little brighter, a square hollow and buzzy, and a sawtooth the brightest and harshest of all.

Station 2

Shape it

The envelope is how loud a sound is from moment to moment. Here it has two parts: the attack, how long the sound takes to rise to full volume, and the length, by when it has died away. A short attack sounds like a strike. A long one sounds like a swell.

Try:

The green is the sound itself, drawn over its length. The amber line is the envelope.

Switch between the two fades and listen to the tail. A straight-line fade seems to hang on and then stop short. Real sounds lose the same fraction of their loudness in each slice of time, which is what an exponential fade does, and your ear hears that as an even fade. Every sound on the bench fades that way.

Station 3

Slide it

Now let the pitch glide while the sound plays. A fast fall reads as something heavy landing. A slow fall is a whistle, something dropping from high up. A rise is power building. The glide is exponential too, so each octave takes the same time.

Try:

The lower picture is a spectrogram: time runs left to right, pitch rises upward, and brighter means louder. Pick a square or sawtooth and you'll see fainter copies above the main line. Those are its overtones, the extra pitches that give a wave its color.

Station 4

Noise

Noise is random values with no repeating shape, so every pitch is in it at once. On its own it's a hiss. Run it through a filter and it becomes something. A lowpass filter keeps the pitches below its cutoff, a rumble. A highpass keeps those above it, a hiss of steam. A bandpass keeps a band around the cutoff, and its Q sets how narrow the band is. Glide the cutoff and the noise moves, like air rushing past.

Noise fills the spectrogram from top to bottom. The filter carves away everything outside the band it keeps.

Station 5

Layers

Real effects stack a few layers that start together. Turn layers off and on, then play again to hear what each one adds. These are the real recipes from Hardpoint.

    The clang works because its four tones are inharmonic. A note on a string has overtones at 2, 3, and 4 times its pitch, and they blend into one note. The clang's sit at about 2.4, 3.5, and 5 times its lowest tone, which never blend, and that's the ring of struck metal. Station 6 lets you change every number in these.

    Station 6

    The bench

    Pick a sound and change anything about it. Each layer is a tone or filtered noise with the controls from the stations above, plus a few more: Start at delays a layer, Vary nudges its pitch at random each time it plays so repeats don't sound mechanical, and Repeat scatters copies of it across a Spread of time, which is how the Arc Lance crackles. Press P anywhere outside a text box to play.

    Things to notice

    • Game sounds are short. Most of Hardpoint's are under a quarter of a second, and the longest, a smoke cloud's hiss, is just over one. A sound only has to say what happened.
    • Direction carries meaning. Pitch that falls reads as weight and impact, and pitch that rises reads as energy building. The mortar's thump and boom both fall, and the lance's charge climbs.
    • Noise does the heavy lifting. Cracks, booms, swings, scratches, and hisses are all filtered noise. Tones mostly add a body under the noise or a ring after it.
    • The attack tells you what made it. A few thousandths of a second reads as a hit. A slower attack, like the swing's 0.07 seconds, reads as something moving through the air.
    • A little randomness goes a long way. The ping's Vary and the crackle's Repeat make every play slightly different, so a burst of three rounds doesn't sound like one sound pasted three times.
    • Small numbers, whole sounds. Every sound here is a short list of numbers. A whole game's worth fits in less space than one second of recorded audio.

    A little history

    • Game consoles built their sounds the same way. The NES's sound chip has five channels: two pulse waves, a triangle, a noise generator, and one channel that plays short samples. Explosions, footsteps, and gunfire on it were mostly the noise channel.
    • sfxr made this a game-jam habit. DrPetter wrote it for the tenth Ludum Dare in December 2007, so entrants racing a 48-hour deadline could get sound effects into their games. Press its randomize button for a new sound, then tweak any parameter by hand. The bench's Random button borrows the idea.
    • Browsers got a synthesizer. The Web Audio API, which wires oscillators, noise, filters, and volume controls together into a graph, became a W3C Recommendation in June 2021. Everything on this page is built from its parts, with no recorded audio at all.
