The Cabinet of Curios

Go and Move 37

Go is two players taking turns putting black and white stones on a grid. Stones never move once they're down, but they can be surrounded and taken off, and whoever surrounds more of the board wins. Work through the first six stations and you'll know enough Go to see why, in 2016, one stone played by a computer program made professionals think it had been put on the wrong point.

Tap a point to play a stone there. Every board also works from the keyboard: Tab to it, move with the arrow keys (Shift moves further), and press Space to play. In whichever station you're using, B takes a move back and N steps forward where there's something to step through. In Station 7, J jumps to just before the famous move.

Station 1

Liberties and capture

The empty points right next to a stone, up, down, left, and right, are its liberties. Stones of one color that touch along the lines form a group and share their liberties. When you fill a group's last liberty, you capture it: every stone in it comes off the board. The number on each stone is its group's liberties, and a dashed red ring means a group is down to one, which is called atari.

Station 2

Ko: no taking straight back

Sometimes a capture leaves the capturing stone in atari itself, and taking it back would put the board exactly as it was. The two players could swap stones forever. So there's one more rule: you may not make a move that puts the whole board back the way it was before. That shape is called a ko. Black captures first. Then try to take back with White, and see what it takes to be allowed.

Station 3

Two eyes live

You can't play a stone where it would have no liberties at all, unless it captures something, because then the capture gives it room. An empty point surrounded by one color is an eye. A group with one eye can be captured: fill its outside liberties, then play in the eye. A group with two separate eyes can never be captured, because you'd have to fill both at once. Play White and try to capture Black.

Station 4

Counting a finished game

The game ends when both players pass in a row. Then each side counts its area: its stones on the board, plus the empty points that touch only its own stones. Stones that are surrounded and can't make two eyes are dead, and come off before counting. White moved second, so White gets komi, extra points to make up for it: 7.5 under the rules AlphaGo and Lee Sedol played by. The half point means there's never a tie. Two stones on this 9 by 9 board are dead. Tap each to take it off and watch the count change.

Professional games often never get this far. A player who counts and sees no way to catch up resigns, and that ends the game on the spot, with no count at all. Both games in Station 7 ended that way.

Station 6

Third line, fourth line, and the shoulder hit

Go players count lines in from the nearest edge: the edge itself is the first line. How far from the edge a stone sits decides what it's good for. Low stones are hard to dislodge and make territory you can count. High stones make less certain territory, but they reach toward the center and help everywhere it's still open. That reach is called influence.

A shoulder hit is a stone played diagonally next to an opponent's stone, on the side facing the center. It sits on the stone's shoulder rather than touching it, so it takes none of its liberties. What it does is get in the way: it stands between that stone and the open middle of the board. Step through one below and see what each side ends up with.

    Move 37

    On 10 March 2016, in a hotel in Seoul, the program AlphaGo played Lee Sedol, one of the strongest players in the world, in the second game of a five-game match. AlphaGo had won the first. This is the real game, move by move, from the first stone. Press Before move 37 to jump ahead, then Next to see it, and read what happened in the room.     Game

    Numbers

    “A beginner plays the corners, an average player the sides; but a master controls the center.”

    The old Chinese saying Fan Hui says move 37 reminded him of, in his commentary on the game.

    Why move 37 was stunning

    • It broke a rule everyone could see. Station 6's crawl is something every strong player has in their bones. A shoulder hit from the fifth line lets the other side run down the fourth line and keep a wide, solid strip of territory. Professionals gave that up only for something big, and here nothing on the board said there was anything big to get. Fan Hui writes that when it came up on the screen, many players thought the stone had been put in the wrong place.
    • It gave up the countable for the uncountable. White's side territory after 37 is easy to count. Black's gain, a loose net over the center that worked with Black's stones along the bottom, isn't. People trust what they can count. AlphaGo had judged the whole board, and it was right: its own estimate of its chances rose over the next few moves, and Black went on to win.
    • AlphaGo knew no human would play it. One part of AlphaGo had been trained on about 30 million positions from games between strong human players, to guess what such a player would do next. That part put the chance of a human choosing 37 at about 1 in 10,000. Another part, which plays games out and judges the results, liked it anyway. So the move was surprising by AlphaGo's own measure, and it played the move regardless.
    • It wasn't even the plan. Fan Hui looked at AlphaGo's data after the match. A few moves earlier it had been planning a jump on the left side (point A at move 37). It found the shoulder hit only after White 36.
    • It came from a machine at the very top. Ten years earlier, programs played Go like decent amateurs. The paper describing AlphaGo calls beating a top professional a feat thought to be at least a decade away. Move 37 wasn't a lucky glitch from a weak program. It was a strong program showing professionals something about Go they hadn't seen.

    Things to notice

    • Stones don't move. Once played, a stone stays put until it's captured. Every bit of strategy on this page comes from where stones are placed, and in what order.
    • A group lives or dies together. Join two stones and they share every liberty. That's why connecting matters so much: two weak stones can become one strong group.
    • Two eyes is the whole secret of life. Station 3's group with two eyes can't be captured by any number of moves, because each eye is a point the other side is never allowed to play. Almost every fight in Go comes down to whether a group can make two eyes.
    • Ko makes fights about tempo. In Station 2, White can retake only after something else on the board changes. In a real game that something is a ko threat: a move elsewhere so dangerous that Black answers it, and then White may take the ko back.
    • Corners are cheapest. In Station 5, walling in 25 points costs 10 stones in a corner, 15 on a side, and 20 in the middle. Both games in Station 7 open with all four corners taken before anything else.
    • Height is a trade. A wall on the fourth line keeps 3 points per row behind it. The same wall on the third line keeps 2. In exchange, the higher wall leaves more room underneath for the other side to slip in. Players weigh that trade on every move.
    • The rules don't say any of this. Station 1's rules, plus ko, are the whole game. Everything about corners, lines, and influence is something players found out over centuries of games, and AlphaGo found some of it out differently.

    A little history

    • An old game. Go began in China more than 2,500 years ago and is mentioned in the Zuo Zhuan, a chronicle from the 4th century BCE. It's called weiqi in Chinese, baduk in Korean, and igo in Japanese, which is where the English name comes from.
    • A hard game for computers. A chess program can look at every move and reply a few turns deep. Go has around 250 legal moves a turn and games last over 200 moves, so that approach drowns. Until 2015 no program had beaten a professional on a full board without a head start.
    • AlphaGo. DeepMind, a London lab owned by Google, built AlphaGo from neural networks, programs that learn patterns from examples, and a search that plays out possible futures. In October 2015 it beat the European champion Fan Hui 5–0. He later joined the team, and his commentary on the Lee Sedol games is what Station 7's notes draw on.
    • The match. AlphaGo played Lee Sedol from 9 to 15 March 2016 at the Four Seasons Hotel in Seoul, under Chinese rules with 7.5 komi and two hours each. AlphaGo won 4–1, and DeepMind says over 200 million people watched.
    • Lee's reply. In Game 4, with the match already lost, Lee played White 78, a wedge into the middle of Black's framework that AlphaGo had also rated as about a 1 in 10,000 move. Gu Li called it the divine move. AlphaGo lost its way and resigned. Choose Game 4 in Station 7 to see it.
    • Afterward. Professionals began copying AlphaGo's ideas, such as invading a corner early at the point next to it. Zhou Ruiyang, a world champion, said players had more or less all been affected by "Professor Alpha." Lee Sedol retired in November 2019, saying that even if he became number one, there was "an entity that cannot be defeated."
    • Another use for the board. John Conway worked out his Game of Life with Go stones on a Go board. Conway's Game of Life puts it back on one.
