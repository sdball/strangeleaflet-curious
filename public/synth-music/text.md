<!-- #intro -->

A lot of synth music is built from the same few pieces: a key, a loop of four chords, and a handful of parts that each play those chords their own way. Hear the chords a key gives you, build a loop from them, then take the loop apart into its parts and play it in four styles. {.lede}

Every station plays out loud, so turn your sound up. No sound on a phone? Check its volume. Each station keeps its own key and chords, so you can change any of them without touching the others. Number and letter keys shown on a button go to the station you're using, and <kbd>P</kbd> starts and stops a loop. {.howto}

<!-- #station-1 -->

A piano's keys climb in **half steps**, from one key to the very next, black or white. Twelve half steps make an octave, where the note names start over. A **key** picks seven of those twelve notes, and those seven are its scale. The marked keys below are the scale of the key you pick, and the first note, its **home**, has the bigger mark.

Build a chord by stacking every other note of the scale: take a note, skip one, take one, skip one, take one. Do that from each of the seven notes and you have the seven chords the key gives you. Musicians number them in Roman numerals by the note they start on. A capital numeral is a **major** chord, which sounds bright. A small one is **minor**, which sounds darker. A small circle marks a **diminished** chord, tense and unsettled.

<!-- #station-1-note -->

Change the key and every chord moves, but the numerals stay with their places in the scale: the first chord is always home, and VI or vi is always built on the sixth note. That's why musicians talk in numerals. A loop written as i–VI–III–VII plays in any minor key. Station 2 builds one. {.note}

<!-- #station-2 -->

Some chords sound like home, and others sound like being away from it. A good rough guide is how many notes a chord shares with the home chord, shown as dots on each chord below. Chords that share two notes sit close to home. Chords that share none feel furthest away.

Press a bar to pick it, then a chord to put there, or press a number key. Each chord lasts one bar, and the loop goes round until you stop it. The presets change the key between major and minor as they need.

<!-- #station-2-note -->

Play Doo-wop, then Synthwave, and listen to the last bar of each as it turns back to the first. Doo-wop ends on V, which holds the note a half step below home. That note pulls up into home so hard that V almost demands I next. In a minor key the seventh note sits a whole step below home, so VII leads back gently. Nothing in the minor loop insists on landing, so it can circle forever, which is a big part of synthwave's dreamy, driving feel. Station 3 dresses a loop like this one up. {.note}

<!-- #station-3 -->

Here's a four-chord loop played as synthwave by five parts at once. Pick its key and chords below, or start from a preset. Each part plays the same four chords in its own way:

- **Drums** keep time: a kick on beats one and three, a snare on two and four, and a hi-hat on every half beat.
- **Bass** plays each chord's root, the note the chord is named after, low and steady. It tells your ear which chord you're on.
- **Chords** hold all three notes of each chord for the whole bar, a soft wash called a pad.
- **Arpeggio** plays the chord's notes one at a time, up and down, four to a beat.
- **Lead** plays a tune made from the chord's notes, over everything else.

Mute a part to hear the loop without it, or solo it to hear it alone. Press a part's letter to mute it, or <kbd>Shift</kbd> and the letter to solo it. The picture shows every note in the loop: time runs left to right across the four bars, and higher notes sit higher in each row.

<!-- #station-3-note -->

Watch the Chords row as the loop goes round. Each note of the pad moves only a step or two to reach the next chord, rather than all three jumping together, which keeps the pad smooth. Musicians call that voice leading. Then listen to the snare: it rings out, then stops dead. A little history, below, says where that sound came from. Station 4 plays a loop in other styles. {.note}

<!-- #station-4 -->

The same four chords could belong to many styles. What changes from one style to the next is mostly the tempo, the drums, the sounds, and what each part does with the chords. Pick a style, or press <kbd>N</kbd> for the next one, and change the loop below it as in Station 3. Tempo is counted in beats per minute, and each bar here is four beats.

<!-- #notes -->

- **The same four chords can make two different loops.** Am, F, C, G in A minor is i–VI–III–VII. Start on C instead and C, G, Am, F is I–V–vi–IV in C major, the Pop preset. Which chord comes first decides where home seems to be.
- **The bass names the chord.** Solo the bass and drums in Station 3 and you can still follow the chords. Much of what your ear needs is the root and the beat.
- **Chiptune has no pad.** It plays its chords one note at a time on the arpeggio, quick enough that you hear the whole chord.
- **Leaving a part out is a choice.** Ambient drops the drums, and chiptune drops the held chords. A part that stops playing can change a style as much as a new one.
- **Tempo moves the mood.** The same chords at 150 beats a minute in chiptune sound busy and bright. At 70 in ambient they sound still.
- **Every loop here is a short list of notes.** Open *The loop as data* to see one: its tempo, then each track's notes as a step, a note name, and how many steps the note lasts, then its instruments. The instruments are built from the same layers as [Sound Bench](../sound-bench/), raised or lowered to the note being played.
- **A real synth does the same jobs.** The [Behringer Wave Guide](../behringer-wave/) holds down a sequenced part on one sound while you play another over it.

<!-- #history -->

- **A synthesizer bassline made a hit.** On Donna Summer's "I Feel Love" (1977), Giorgio Moroder's bass came from a Moog synthesizer locked to a pulse recorded on the tape, so every later part played in perfect time with it. Brian Eno said that year that the single would change the sound of club music for the next fifteen years.
- **The 1980s snare came from a stray microphone.** In 1979, recording Peter Gabriel's third album at London's Townhouse Studios with Phil Collins on drums, engineer Hugh Padgham opened a talkback microphone and ran it through a noise gate, which shuts a sound off once it falls quiet. The room's ring cut off short. Collins's "In the Air Tonight" made the gated sound famous.
- **The NES had three channels for notes.** Its sound chip has five channels: two pulse waves, a triangle, a noise channel, and one for recorded samples. With three notes at most, the chiptune style here plays its chords one note at a time instead of holding them.
- **Ambient music was named on a record sleeve.** Brian Eno's notes for *Ambient 1: Music for Airports*, released in February 1979, say ambient music "must be as ignorable as it is interesting."
