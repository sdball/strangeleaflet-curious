The Cabinet of Curios

Z-Machine Interpreter

Infocom wrote its games for a computer that didn't exist, the Z-machine, and shipped a small program for each real computer that pretended to be one. This page is one of those programs. It loads a story file and plays it: you type, the game answers.

Type a command and press Enter: look, take leaflet, go north or just n. ↑ and ↓ bring back what you typed before. save and restore keep games in this browser, and Undo takes back a move. Saves lists the games you've saved: restore one, rename it, delete it, or download it as a file that other interpreters open, with your notes and map inside. Open a save file there, or drop one on the page, to add it. When a save brings notes or a map, Restore asks whether to bring them back too. Notes opens a notepad beside the game for clues and combinations, and Map draws the rooms you walk through as you go, with the things you've seen in each, and Redraw tidies it from every way you've found. In the three Zorks, Enhanced adds a line under the status line with what the game keeps to itself: whether the room has light of its own, turns left on the lamp you're carrying, how much more you can carry, how long your wounds take to heal, and in Zork I, once you've opened the trophy case, the treasures in it. The Zorks also get Cheats: Well lit gives every room light, and in Zork I, No thief keeps the thief out of the game and Stable objects stops him taking what you leave lying around. Inspector shows what the machine holds as you play: the object tree, every global variable, and what each command changed, named the way ZILF named them in the built-in stories. It starts with only the room you're in, and Whole tree shows the rest, hidden things too. Type in Find an object to pick out things by name or number, and see what each is inside. In full screen, Saves, Notes, Map, and Inspector are on the right end of the screen's top line. x works for examine in every game. Full screen gives the story the whole window, with nothing else on it, and Esc brings you back. Pick a screen to play on: a desk monitor, a green screen, an Atari 800, or a printout on fanfold paper. The page remembers where you were, so you can close it and come back.

      Things to notice

      • The interpreter doesn't understand a word you type. It turns your line to lower case, splits it at spaces and at the few punctuation marks the game lists as separators, and looks each piece up in the game's dictionary. The game gets back a list: where each word sits in its dictionary, how long it is, and where it was in your line. Working out what put the crank in the socket means is the game's own code.
      • Only the start of a long word counts. A Version 3 game keeps six Z-characters of each dictionary word, and Version 4 and later keep nine. So in After Hours, examine odometry examines the odometer, since both pack to odomet.
      • In Version 3, the status line is the interpreter's job. The game only keeps the current room in its first global variable, and the score and moves in the next two. The interpreter draws the bar from those just before it reads a line, or when the game asks with show_status. From Version 4 on, games draw their own status line in an upper window.
      • The map knows where you are from the status line. In Version 3 it reads the same global the status line does. Later games draw their own status line, so the map takes the first object a game names there, which is the room in Inform and ZIL games. When the room changes after a command, the map draws that command as the way between the two rooms. When a direction leaves you where you were and the game describes the room again, as in Zork's maze, the map draws a small loop on that side of the room. A way you can't take says so without naming the room, so it draws nothing. A game that writes its room name some other way gets no map. Things go on the map once the game names them, or prints one of their own descriptions, while they're in your room, so whatever is still hidden stays off it.
      • Enhanced reads what the Zorks keep to themselves. The games know whether a room has light of its own, how long your lamp has left, how much more you can carry, and how long your wounds take to heal, and only hint at them: “It is pitch black.” once your light is gone, “The lamp appears a bit dimmer.”, “Your load is too heavy.”, or the numbers diagnose gives in Zork I. The line under the status line reads them from the game's memory each turn. A room with light of its own carries a flag, ONBIT, and the game checks it before looking for anything lit you carry, so the line can tell you a room is dark while your lamp is on, or lit well enough to turn the lamp off and save it. Lights come from the clock, a table holding every timer: whether it's running, how many turns until it goes off, and which routine it calls then. The lamp's routine runs at each stage of a second table (in Zork I, 200 turns, then 100, 70, and 15), so the turns left are the turns to the next stage plus every stage after it, and turning the lamp off stops the clock. Load adds up the size of everything you carry, the way the game does before it lets you take something, and the limit drops while you're hurt. The page finds all this by the names ZILF lists when it builds each game, so Enhanced comes only with the built-in Zorks. Any command the game understands takes a turn, even walking into a wall, and wait takes three. Only a few, like save, score, and verbose, are free.
      • A cheat answers for one of the game's own routines. Each time the game calls a routine, the interpreter checks its address, and when a cheat that's on covers that routine, it hands back an answer without running any of it. Zork asks a routine called LIT? whether a room has light, and Well lit always answers yes, the same answer the game gives in Zork I once you've died and wander as a spirit. The game's clock calls I-THIEF every turn to move the thief and let him rob, and No thief answers for it, so he never moves. Then the page does what the game does when he slips away and when he gets home: he goes out of sight, and any treasures he was carrying wait in his Treasure Room. Stable objects answers for the three routines that take things from a room, and leaves alone the one that robs you. Turn a cheat off and those routines run again. Only the thief opens the jeweled egg without breaking it. To keep the canary whole, turn No thief off once you've found the egg: he starts out again from where he stood, and a treasure you carry or leave lying is his to take home and open.
      • One command is thousands of instructions. Typing take leaflet at the start of Zork I runs about 1,650 of them, through 37 routines: the parser matching your words, the checks on whether you can reach the leaflet, the take itself, and then each timer and creature on the game's clock, the thief among them. In the Inspector, This turn lists the routines in the order they were first called, and what the command moved, switched, and changed.
      • x means examine, even where the game never heard of it. The Zorks want examine lamp or look at lamp, and many players now type x lamp without thinking. When a game's dictionary has no x, the interpreter passes examine in its place at the start of each command. The game itself is unchanged.
      • A game can keep time while you type. From Version 4, a game asking for a line or a key can also name a routine and a time, in tenths of a second. Each time that runs out, the interpreter calls the routine while your half-typed line waits. The routine may print something, and you carry on typing below it, or it may end your line where it stands. A Version 5 game can then hand back what you'd typed, and this page puts it back in the input for you to finish.
      • Each screen shows colours its own way. From Version 5 a game can ask for eight named colours, or any of 32,768 more. The desk shows them as the Standard lists them, and the Atari 800 uses its own palette, with its own blue. The green screen shows the same colours with a touch of its green mixed in. The printout prints letters in coloured inks and leaves backgrounds as paper, since a ribbon can't print white. A game that clears the screen in its colours gets the whole screen in them, the way Photopia changes colour from scene to scene.
      • A save is small. Everything a game can change sits in its dynamic memory, so a save needs only that, the stack, and where the game was in its code. This page stores only the bytes that differ from the story file as it was loaded, and says how many bytes each save took.
      • A save file works in other interpreters. Download in Saves writes a Quetzal file, the save format Frotz and many other interpreters read and write. It says which story it's for by release, serial number, and checksum, then holds dynamic memory packed the same way this page keeps it, and the call stack, oldest call first. The page adds the save's name, your notes, and your map in chunks of its own, and other interpreters skip chunks they don't know. Open the file on another computer, phone, or tablet, and the save joins the list there. Restore it, and you choose whether its notes and map replace the ones there. A save for one of the built-in stories opens that story first.
      • Restart doesn't reload the file. The game can't change static or high memory, so restarting copies the story file's dynamic memory back over the changed copy and starts at the first instruction again. Only the transcript and fixed-pitch bits of the header survive.

      Where to find story files

      People still write games for the Z-machine, and hundreds are free to download. The IF Archive keeps them.

      • The IF Archive's z-code folder lists them by file name, each with a line about the game.
      • IFDB, the Interactive Fiction Database, finds games by title, author, or subject. A game's page links to its download.

      Files ending .z1 to .z5, .z7 and .z8 play here; .z3, .z5 and .z8 are the usual ones. A .zblorb wraps a story file with its cover art or sounds, and this page takes the story file out. Version 6 games (.z6) draw pictures and lay out their own windows, which this page doesn't do. Files ending .ulx or .gblorb are for Glulx, a different machine.

      A few to start with, all on the IF Archive:

      • 9:05 by Adam Cadre, a few minutes long.
      • Photopia by Adam Cadre, first entered in the 1998 IF Competition.
      • Lost Pig by Admiral Jota, winner of the 2007 IF Competition.
      • Spider and Web by Andrew Plotkin, from 1998. Its file is called Tangle.z5.
      • The Dreamhold by Andrew Plotkin, written to teach newcomers how to play.
      • Curses by Graham Nelson, release 16 from 1995.

      Most of Infocom's games are still under copyright, so this page doesn't link to copies. If you have the files from a copy you own, they play here like any other. Zork I, II, and III are the exception: their source is free under the MIT License, at historicalsource/zork1, zork2, and zork3.
