The Cabinet of Curios

Pixel Bench

A pixel-art creature can be a few dozen colored squares and still read at a glance as a dolphin, a jellyfish, or a glowing anglerfish. Work through the stations to see how each trick works, then open the bench and redraw any creature in Cozy Submarine or Pocket Places.

Every picture here is drawn from the same data the game uses. Click or tap the buttons to switch creatures, and change anything you like: nothing here touches the game itself. To draw your own sub, go to Custom Submarine.

Station 1

A grid of letters

This is Cozy Submarine's crab, and beside it is the crab as the game stores it: four lines of text, one letter per pixel. A short list, the palette, says which color each letter means. A space is empty water. Change a letter in the text and the picture follows. Click a square, or press Space on one, to step it through the colors, and the text follows.

Arrow keys move between squares. Space steps the square to the next color.

    The game draws it this size, one screen square per letter (a few real screen pixels each):

    Art always faces right. When the crab walks left, the game reads each row backward. Station 2 is about how so few squares can still say "crab".

    Station 2

    Small on purpose

    At the size the game draws them, there's no room for detail, so the outline does almost all the work. A tall fin says shark, a beak says dolphin, a flat shell with flippers says turtle. Press Outline only to paint each creature in one color and see if you still know it.

    Up close, the pixels look crude. Small, your eye fills in the curves. Pixel artists check their work at its real size for exactly that reason.

    Station 3

    Lines and jaggies

    On a grid, a straight line is a staircase. When every step is the same length, your eye reads it as a clean line. When the steps go long, short, long, it looks bumpy, a look called jaggies. Slide the rise and watch the steps.

    Try a rise of 12, 8, or 6: one up for every two, three, or four across, and every step matches. Pixel artists lean on those slopes. For a curve, they let the steps grow or shrink steadily instead of jumping around.

    Station 4

    Smoothing edges

    A curve has the same stairs. Artists soften them by hand: where a step juts out, they add a pixel of an in-between color, partway from the shape to what's behind it. Up close it looks blurry. At real size, the circle looks rounder. This is called anti-aliasing.

    The in-between colors are mixed with the background the art was drawn for. Switch to the dark background and they turn into a pale fringe. That's why sprites that move over many backgrounds, like the creatures in Cozy Submarine, keep hard edges.

    Station 5

    Swap the palette

    The letters say where each color goes, and the palette says what it is. Change the palette and every pixel with that letter changes at once, without redrawing anything. Real clownfish come in all three of these colorings, so one drawing covers them all. Then mix your own.

    One drawing, four birds

    Pocket Places goes further. Every small bird in it is the same drawing, perched, pecking, and flying, and each kind brings only its own palette. Change the drawing once and every bird changes with it.

    Cozy Submarine does this while you play. The sub's light lenses are their own letters, and the game paints them grey when a light is off and white when it's on.

    Station 6

    Light and shade

    A shape in one flat color looks like a cutout. Add a lighter shade where light would hit and a darker one where it wouldn't, and it turns round. Here each creature is drawn twice: once with every body color made the same, and once as the game draws it.

    Look at the horseshoe crab: a few pale squares where light catches the top of the shell and the tip of the tail, a middle brown for the shell, a darker band down the tail, and the darkest brown for the rim and legs underneath. All browns, and the shell reads as a dome lit from above.

    A ramp of shades

    The shades for one material make a ramp. The plain way to build one is to darken a single color. Many artists also turn the hue as they go: shadows lean cooler, toward purple and blue, and highlights lean warmer, toward yellow. Flip between the two ramps on the same ball.

    Station 7

    Dithering

    With only a few colors, a smooth fade turns into stripes, called banding. Dithering mixes two neighboring colors in a fine pattern, more of one at one end and more of the other at the far end. Up close you see the checks. At a distance your eye blends them into shades the palette doesn't have.

    None of the curios here dither, since their creatures are tiny and move a lot. It earns its keep in skies, water, and other large shapes.

    Station 8

    Two frames

    Motion is a second drawing, swapped in and out. The creatures in Cozy Submarine that move do it with just two frames, and most of their pixels stay put. Press Mark the changes to tint the ones that differ between the frames pink.

    Speed matters as much as the drawing. The crab flips its legs six times a second and scuttles. The moon jelly swaps one and a half times a second and pulses.

    Station 9

    In the dark

    The same pixels look different depending on where they are. Cozy Submarine dims everything as you go down, the way the sea swallows sunlight. Two marks on a palette color change that. A color marked to glow keeps its brightness in the dark. A see-through color is half its own color and half the water behind it. Drag the depth and watch which pixels survive.

    0 m

    Deep-sea animals really do this. Many of them make their own light, and many have see-through bodies with almost no color to hide.

    Station 10

    The bench

    Pick any creature and change anything about it: its pixels, its colors and their marks, its size, and its frames. Click or drag to paint, and right-click to rub out. On the grid, the arrow keys move a cursor, Space paints, and Delete rubs out. P pencil, F fill, I picker, 0–9 pick a color, [ ] change frame, Ctrl Z undo.

    Arrow keys move the cursor, Shift moves four at a time. Space or Enter uses the tool, Delete rubs out.

    Width Height Move

    Width grows and shrinks on the right, at the nose. Height grows and shrinks at the top, so anything that walks on the floor keeps its feet. Moving shifts every frame together.

    Things to notice

    • A whole sea is a few pages of text. Every creature in Cozy Submarine, frames and all, fits in less space than one small photo. That's what makes it easy to copy, change, and paste back.
    • The outline carries the name. At the game's size you mostly see a silhouette. Fins, tails, and beaks are where the pixels are spent.
    • Colors live in the palette, not the pixels. One drawing can be many creatures, and a game can recolor things while you play for free.
    • Even steps make clean lines. One up for every one, two, or three across reads as straight. Anything in between shows its stairs.
    • Smoothing belongs to its background. Hand-placed in-between colors round off a curve, but only against the color they were mixed with.
    • Two or three shades make a curve. A light row on top and a dark row underneath, and a flat shape gains a body and a light source.
    • A few colors can pass for many. A dithered pattern of two colors reads, from a step back, as the color between them.
    • Motion is mostly stillness. A few pixels flipping between two frames is enough, as long as the speed suits the animal.
    • Where it is changes what it looks like. Glowing and see-through colors let the same art read in sunlit shallows and pitch-black depths.

    A little history

    • Game consoles worked in tiny tiles. The NES draws its moving objects as sprites of 8 × 8 pixels (or 8 × 16), up to 64 at once. Each sprite uses one of four sprite palettes, and each palette holds three colors plus see-through, chosen from 64. Larger characters are several sprites side by side, and because the colors live in the palette, the same tiles can be drawn in another one.
    • Plotters drew in steps too. In 1965 Jack Bresenham published a way for a computer to choose which steps a digital plotter's pen should take to draw a straight line, using only whole-number arithmetic. It's still the classic way to draw a line on a grid.
    • Dither patterns came from printing pictures in two tones. In 1973 B. E. Bayer described an ordered pattern for showing shades of gray with dots that are only on or off. The dithering station uses his 4 × 4 pattern.
    • The first Macintosh icons were drawn on graph paper. Susan Kare designed them for the Mac that launched in January 1984, coloring in squares on the smallest graph paper she could find, mostly at 32 × 32 and in black and white. The Museum of Modern Art acquired her sketchbooks in 2015.
