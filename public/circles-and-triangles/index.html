The Cabinet of Curios

Circles and Triangles

Draw a circle with a radius of 1 and fit a right triangle inside it, with its long side along the radius. Turn the radius and the other two sides change length. Those lengths are the sine and the cosine, and the same circle gives four more: tangent, secant, cosecant, and cotangent. Turn all the way round and the triangle unrolls into a wave.

Drag the point P round each circle, or Tab to it and use the arrow keys: each press turns 1°, and Shift turns 15°. Every button and slider works from the keyboard too.

Station 1

A triangle in a circle

Draw a circle of radius 1 around a point O. Put a point P on it, turned an angle θ (the Greek letter theta) up from the x-axis. Drop a line straight down from P to the x-axis and you have a right triangle: one corner is square, and the long side, the radius, is 1. The sine of θ, written sin θ, is the triangle's height. The cosine, cos θ, is its width. Together they say where P is: cos θ across and sin θ up.

Station 2

Any size

Most right triangles don't have a long side of 1. A ladder leaning on a wall makes one with the ladder as its long side, called the hypotenuse. It's Station 1's triangle scaled up by the ladder's length L, so each side is L times as long. The top reaches L × sin θ up the wall, and the foot stands L × cos θ out from it.

Divide back and you get the way sine and cosine are usually taught. The opposite side is across from θ, the adjacent side runs alongside it, and sin θ = opposite ÷ hypotenuse, cos θ = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse. Drag the foot of the ladder, or change its length. Station 3 takes the third ratio, opposite ÷ adjacent.

Station 3

The tangent line

Draw a line straight up through the point where the circle crosses the x-axis. It touches the circle there without crossing it, so it's called a tangent line, from the Latin for touching. Carry the radius on past P until it meets that line. The height where they meet is the tangent of θ, tan θ. The big triangle under it is the small one scaled up until its width is 1, so tan θ = sin θ ÷ cos θ, which is opposite ÷ adjacent. It's also the slope of the radius: how far it climbs for every 1 across.

Station 4

All the way round

Station 1 stopped at 90°. Keep turning and P's width and height can point the other way. Count them like coordinates: a width to the left of the y-axis is negative, and so is a height below the x-axis. Then (cos θ, sin θ) is exactly where P is, at any angle. The triangle inside is always a first-quarter triangle, flipped or turned, so the sizes repeat and only the signs change. Its two short sides and the radius make a right triangle, so Pythagoras says sin² θ + cos² θ = 1 at every angle, where sin² θ means (sin θ)².

Station 5

The other three

Now draw the tangent line through P itself, at right angles to the radius. It crosses the x-axis at a point S and the y-axis at a point C, and the pieces of this one picture give six functions. tan θ is the stretch of tangent line from P to S, and the cotangent, cot θ, is the stretch from P to C. The secant, sec θ, runs from O to S, and the cosecant, csc θ, from O to C. Each new one is a ratio of the first three: sec θ = 1 ÷ cos θ, csc θ = 1 ÷ sin θ, and cot θ = cos θ ÷ sin θ. Pick one to pick out its line and see where its ratio comes from.

Station 6

Unroll the circle

Back to sine. Walk P round the circle and carry its height across to a graph. The graph uses the same scale as the circle, so when P has walked some distance round, the graph has moved that same distance to the right: the circle is unrolled flat. The height traces a wave, the sine wave.

That walk is a better way to measure an angle than degrees. An angle in radians is how far you walk round a circle of radius 1. A whole turn is the whole way round, 2π ≈ 6.283, so 360° is 2π radians, 180° is π, and 90° is π/2. Press Radians to switch. Turn on cos θ and you'll see the same wave, a quarter turn ahead.

Things to notice

  • Three angles have exact values worth knowing. sin 30° = ½, sin 45° = √2 ÷ 2 ≈ 0.707, and sin 60° = √3 ÷ 2 ≈ 0.866, and cos runs through the same three numbers backwards. Cut a square along its diagonal for 45°, and cut a triangle with three equal sides in half for 30° and 60°. Try them in Station 1.
  • "Co" means the other angle. The two small corners of a right triangle add up to 90°, so the side opposite one corner is adjacent to the other. That makes cos θ = sin(90° − θ), and the same goes for cot and tan, and for csc and sec. 90° − θ is called the complement of θ, and the cosine is the complement's sine.
  • For small angles, sin θ is nearly θ. In radians, sin 0.1 = 0.0998 and tan 0.1 = 0.1003. A short walk round the circle is almost a straight climb, so the height you gain is about the distance you walked. Exploring e leans on this in its Station 3: a small sideways push turns you by about its own size, in radians.
  • The 1 in 4 rule is a cosine. US workplace safety rules ask for a ladder's foot to stand about a quarter of its working length from the wall. That fixes cos θ at 1 ÷ 4, so every ladder set up that way leans at about 75.5°, whatever its length. Going from a ratio back to its angle is the job of the inverse functions, written arccos or cos−1.
  • A point on the circle is a turn. (cos θ, sin θ) is where the point 1 lands when you turn it by θ about O. In Complex Numbers that's the number cos θ + i sin θ, and its Station 6 turns the axes with a table of cosines and sines. Exploring e wraps the number line round this same circle in its Station 6, which is the unrolling of Station 6 here, run backwards.
  • sec and csc obey Pythagoras too. In Station 5 the triangle O, P, S has short sides 1 and tan θ and long side sec θ, so 1 + tan² θ = sec² θ. The triangle O, P, C gives 1 + cot² θ = csc² θ the same way.

A little history

  • Chords came first. Greek astronomers worked with chords: straight lines across a circle from one point on it to another. The first known table of chords was made by Hipparchus in about 140 BC, and Ptolemy's, in the second century, went up in steps of half a degree. A chord across an angle of 2θ is twice sin θ. Reflect Station 1's triangle in the x-axis and the line from P to its mirror image is that chord.
  • Half chords. In about 500, Aryabhata gave tables of half chords, which really are sine tables, and called the half chord jya.
  • A word that meant nothing. Arabic astronomers took up jya as jiba, a word with no meaning that sounded like it. Later writers turned it into jaib, which does mean something: a fold. Translators into Latin rendered jaib as sinus, the Latin for a fold, and sinus became sine.
  • Shadows. The first known tables of shadows were made by Arab astronomers around 860, for two kinds of shadow later called umbra recta and umbra versa in Latin. Shadows are tangents and cotangents. With the sun θ above the horizon, a stick of length 1 standing upright casts a shadow cot θ long, and a stick of length 1 jutting straight out from a wall casts a shadow tan θ down the wall.
  • Naming the lines. Thomas Fincke's Geometriae rotundi, published in Basel in 1583, introduced the words tangent and secant. Copernicus had known the secant, and called it the hypotenusa.
  • The co- functions. Edmund Gunter suggested co-sinus in 1620 and was the first to use cotangens. The short form cot came from Jonas Moore in 1674.
