The Cabinet of Curios

Fibonacci and Lucas Sequences

Start with 1 and 1. Make each new number by adding the last two: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. Those are the Fibonacci numbers. Build them out of squares, watch their ratios settle on one number, then turn two dials to change the rule and find a whole family, the Lucas sequences. Some of them grow, and some go round in circles.

Every button, dial, and slider works from the keyboard. In Station 1, + adds a square and − takes one away.

Station 1

Add the last two

Start with a 1 by 1 square and put a second one beside it. Keep adding squares, each one along the longest side of the rectangle so far. That side is made of the last two squares laid end to end, so each new square's side is the sum of the last two: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. These are the Fibonacci numbers, written F1, F2, F3, and so on. A quarter circle in each square joins up into a spiral.

Station 2

The ratio settles

Divide each Fibonacci number by the one before it: 2 ÷ 1 = 2, 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5, 5 ÷ 3 = 1.667, 8 ÷ 5 = 1.6. The ratios hop from one side to the other, closer every time, and settle on 1.6180339…, the golden ratio, written φ (the Greek letter phi). Here's why. 13 ÷ 8 = (8 + 5) ÷ 8 = 1 + 5 ÷ 8, so each ratio is 1 plus 1 over the ratio before it. Once the ratios stop moving, φ has to equal 1 + 1/φ. Multiply both sides by φ and φ × φ = φ + 1. The only positive number that does that is (1 + √5) ÷ 2.

● Fn+1 ÷ Fn ╌ φ

Station 3

Same rule, new start

The rule says how to get the next number, not where to start. Run Fibonacci backward from 1, 1 and you get 1 − 1 = 0, so F0 = 0 and the sequence can start 0, 1. Start from 2, 1 instead and you get the Lucas numbers: 2, 1, 3, 4, 7, 11, 18. They're named for Édouard Lucas, who studied both. Pick any two starting numbers and the ratios still settle on φ, because every sequence made with this rule is built from Fibonacci numbers. Start from a and b, and term n is a × Fn−1 + b × Fn: two copies of Fibonacci, one a step behind the other.

● your ratios ● Fibonacci's ╌ φ

Station 4

Turn the dials

Fibonacci's rule adds the last two terms. Lucas studied a whole family of rules with two dials, P and Q: multiply the last term by P, multiply the one before it by Q, and subtract. xn = Pxn−1 − Qxn−2. Fibonacci is P = 1, Q = −1, since subtracting −1 times a number adds it. Each setting makes two sequences: U starts 0, 1 like Fibonacci, and V starts 2, P like Lucas.

The ratio settles for the same reason as in Station 2. Once it stops at some number r, each term is r times the last, so r × r = P × r − Q. That's the equation x² − Px + Q = 0, and it usually has two answers, r1 and r2. Every term is a mix of their powers. In fact Vn = r1n + r2n exactly. With Fibonacci's dials the answers are φ and −0.618, and 1.6185 + (−0.618)5 = 11.09 − 0.09 = 11, the Lucas number V5. Raise them to higher and higher powers and the answer farther from 0 wins, so the ratio settles on it.

Station 5

When it doesn't settle

Some dials give an equation with no answer on the number line. P = 1, Q = 1 asks for r × r = r − 1, and no number does that. In the plane of complex numbers it has two answers, 0.5 + 0.866i and 0.5 − 0.866i, mirror images across the number line. Multiplying by a complex number turns and stretches, so the powers of r walk round 0 instead of out along the line, and the sequences follow them.

Station 4 found Vn = rn + (its mirror image)n. Add a point to its mirror image and the heights cancel while the distances along the number line double. So V is twice the walking point's shadow on the number line, and U is its height, counted in steps of r's own height. At P = 1, Q = 1 each step turns 60° and doesn't stretch at all, so after 6 steps the point is home and both sequences repeat.

Things to notice

  • Lucas numbers are powers of φ, rounded. φ5 = 11.09 and the Lucas number L5 is 11. φ10 = 122.99 and L10 = 123. That's Station 4's Vn = r1n + r2n at work: the other answer, −0.618, shrinks toward 0 with every power, so from L2 on it never moves φn as much as half a step. Fibonacci numbers are rounded too: Fn is φn ÷ √5, rounded to the nearest whole number.
  • Neighbors take turns being too big and too small. 5 × 13 = 65 and 8 × 8 = 64. 8 × 21 = 168 and 13 × 13 = 169. Multiply the Fibonacci numbers on either side of one and you always land 1 away from its square, alternately above and below. That's why the ratios in Station 2 hop from one side of φ to the other.
  • U sequences divide neatly. Whenever m divides n, Um divides Un. F3 = 2 divides F6 = 8 and F9 = 34. F5 = 5 divides F10 = 55. For the Mersenne dials, 23 − 1 = 7 divides 26 − 1 = 63. So a Mersenne number 2n − 1 can only be prime when n is prime, and a Fibonacci number bigger than 3 can only be prime when its position is prime.
  • Pell numbers find √2. With P = 2, Q = −1, halve each V and divide by the U beside it: 3/2, 7/5, 17/12, 41/29, 99/70. Each is closer to √2 = 1.41421… than any fraction with a smaller bottom. 99/70 = 1.41429.
  • The dials don't have to be whole numbers. Lucas worked with whole numbers so every term is a whole number too, but the rule works for any P and Q. The answers to x² − Px + Q = 0 still decide everything: which one wins, how fast, and whether the sequence grows, shrinks, or turns.

A little history

  • Poets counted them first. Sanskrit verse measures lines in short syllables, one beat each, and long ones, two beats. Count the rhythms that fill n beats: each one starts with a short syllable and then fills n − 1 beats, or a long one and then fills n − 2. So the counts add the last two: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8. Virahāṅka gave the rule some time between 600 and 800, and Gopāla (before 1135) and Hemacandra (about 1150) gave it again, all before Fibonacci.
  • Fibonacci's rabbits. In his Liber abaci of 1202, Leonardo of Pisa, later called Fibonacci, walled in a pair of rabbits. Every month each pair bears a new pair, which starts breeding in its second month. How many pairs after a year? Counting month by month, he added the last two each time and reached 377.
  • Lucas gave them their name. Édouard Lucas was the first to call the sequence after Fibonacci, in the 1870s. In 1878 he published a long paper on the whole family, writing the rule with the roots of x² = Px − Q and calling the two sequences Un and Vn, the letters used here.
  • A prime found by hand. In 1876 Lucas used these sequences to prove that 2127 − 1, a number with 39 digits, is prime. It's still the largest prime ever found without a computer.
  • Lehmer's test. In 1930 Derrick Henry Lehmer extended Lucas's theory and turned his method into a short test for any Mersenne number 2p − 1 with p an odd prime. Start at 4 and keep squaring and subtracting 2: 4, 14, 194, 37634. Those are V1, V2, V4, and V8 for P = 4, Q = 1 (try them in Station 4). 2p − 1 is prime exactly when it divides the number at position p − 2, counting the 4 as position 0. 31 = 25 − 1 divides 37634, which is 31 × 1214, so 31 is prime.
  • Binet's formula wasn't Binet's first. Jacques Binet published Fn = (φn − (−0.618…)n) ÷ √5 in 1843, but Abraham de Moivre had found it in 1718.
