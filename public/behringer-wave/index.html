Two digital oscillators per voice produce the raw waveforms, get converted to analog, then pass through a voltage-controlled low-pass filter and amplifier, exactly as on any analog polysynth. Eight of these run in parallel.

Behringer printed the division on the panel itself. The left two-thirds is the analog control panel: one knob per parameter, each continuously variable. The right-hand third is the digital control panel: a keypad, a display and a set of menus, since the digital parameters are mostly mode switches with no useful in-between positions. Learning the instrument is largely learning which half a given idea lives in.

What a wavetable actually is

A wavetable is neither a sample nor a single waveform. It's an ordered list of 64 single-cycle waveforms, each 128 samples long, stored in a fixed sequence. The Wave holds 30 factory tables plus 64 user slots.

You don't shape a waveform here the way you'd set a pulse width or blend a saw and a square. You choose a table, then choose a numbered position in it. That's what the WAVES-OSC and WAVES-SUB knobs do. Everything expressive follows from modulating that position while a note sounds.

Think of a wavetable as a route rather than a sound. The tables are arranged so that travelling through them is musical: harmonics stacking up in order, dark to bright and back, or a series of vowel formants. Choosing a table is choosing what kind of journey a note can take. Two things follow:

  • Movement through the table is stepped, not continuous. Adjacent waveforms are switched abruptly and the phase discontinuity produces a click or a rasp. That artefact is the sound of early wavetable hardware, and it's optional: the OSC setting in the User menu morphs between adjacent waves instead.
  • The last four positions of every table, 60 to 63, hold sawtooth, pulse, square and triangle. They're there so you can use the Wave as a conventional analog synth, and they're a nuisance when you sweep through them, which is what the UW setting exists to manage.

Slots 31 to 63 hold transients instead. These are short samples, and there the same knobs set playback start and loop points rather than table positions.

What moves the position

Three envelopes and an LFO, with fixed default assignments rather than a free modulation matrix. Depth for each is set by its own knob.

What an envelope is, and what ADSR and AD mean

An envelope generator produces a control voltage that rises and falls each time you press a key, used to make a parameter change over the life of a note. Two of the Wave’s three have four stages:

AAttackHow long to climb from nothing to full after the key goes down.
DDecayHow long to fall from full down to the sustain level.
SSustainThe level it holds while the key stays down. Unlike the other three, it isn't a time.
RReleaseHow long to fall from there to nothing after you let go.
The two stages depend on each other

Decay does nothing while Sustain sits at maximum. Decay is the fall to the sustain level, so if sustain is already full there is nowhere to fall and the decay time has no effect. And Release does nothing once the envelope has already reached zero, because release fades from wherever the envelope currently is. Set Sustain to 00 to make decay audible and you remove the level that release needs. To hear all four stages at once, put Sustain somewhere in the middle, around 40, with a moderate Decay and a long Release.

That’s an ADSR envelope, and it’s what Envelopes 1 and 2 are. Envelope 3 is an AD envelope: attack and decay only, no sustain level and no release, so it always rises once and falls back regardless of how long you hold the key. In place of the missing controls it has an attenuation knob setting how far it travels.

Applied to loudness an envelope is a volume shape. Applied to filter cutoff or wave position it’s a timbral shape: the same four numbers, now describing a journey through the wavetable instead of a change in level. That reuse is the whole idea behind Lesson 03.

ENV and EG mean the same thing

Behringer’s panel and manual use both abbreviations for envelope generator, in different places. Beyond those, wave position can also be driven by the keyboard, the mod wheel, the bend wheel, aftertouch and an expression pedal. All of it is switched on in the Digital menu with two-letter codes: source letter, then destination letter.

The whole voice, including modulation

The audio path along the bottom is the same one from the top of this page. Above each stage sits everything that can modulate it. Taken together, that's the complete picture of what the Digital and Tuning menus are for.

Read it as three destinations. Wave position and pitch shape what the oscillators produce; cutoff shapes what survives the filter; loudness shapes what comes out. Almost every source can reach all three, and the two-letter code is just which pair you’re connecting.

The envelope strip along the bottom is worth reading closely, because Envelope 3 is the odd one out. Envelopes 1 and 2 have destinations they always drive; Envelope 3 has none until you send it somewhere, and there are only two places it can go. That makes it the instrument’s second gesture. Everything else moves on Envelope 1’s timing, and EG3 is how you get something moving on its own clock.

Envelope 2 gates everything else

Nothing is audible unless the amplifier is open, so Envelope 2's release time sets how much of any other envelope's tail you get to hear. A filter movement or wave sweep that plays out after you lift a key is silent if EG2 Release is at 00. Whenever an effect seems not to happen after release, check R2 before anything else.

The division of labour

The single most useful idea for programming this instrument: the digital half decides which harmonics exist, the analog half decides which of them you keep. If a patch sounds thin, more cutoff won't help, because you can't filter in harmonics that were never generated. Move the wave position instead.

One last piece of terminology: every program contains two sounds, Group A and Group B, which can be layered, split across the keyboard or used one at a time. These lessons use Group A only.

How to work these lessons

Three practical notes, then straight into it.

Nothing here gets saved

Every edit you make goes straight into the edit buffer and you hear it immediately. Saving is only for keeping a sound; it isn't needed to work or to hear results. So none of these lessons ask you to store anything, and each one ends with the values to put back before moving on.

Three things do wipe the edit buffer, and they're your reset options rather than hazards:

Load any programEnter a different program number, then come back. The buffer refills from stored data, giving you a clean slate to rebuild the base patch from.
DTF 8 (INIT)Resets the Wave to factory settings. Confirm with 1, cancel with 0. It does not erase user wavetables or transients. Safe to use if you've never saved anything, and the fastest way out of a patch you've mangled.
Power offSame effect. Nothing you've been doing survives, which is exactly what you want here.

If you do end up with a sound you want to keep: Program menu → cursor to DTF → 9 → pick bank → cursor to PG → pick a program number → 1 to confirm.

Knobs or keypad, whichever is quicker

The Analog menu shows all seventeen panel controls as numbers from 00 to 63, and turning a knob updates its number live. So either method hits an exact value: watch the display and turn, or cursor to the parameter and type. Knobs are better for hearing a value sweep past; the keypad is better for jumping straight to a number, and it's the only way to reach the Digital and Tuning parameters.

The one thing to know: a knob's physical position overwrites the stored value the instant you move it. That only matters when you recall a saved patch and expect its stored values back, which these lessons never do.

Single-digit entries on the keypad need a leading zero: 04, not 4.

How exact do the numbers need to be?

Mostly not very. The values here are a starting point that produces the character being described, not a recipe that breaks if you miss by four. Values fall into two groups, and it helps to know which one you're looking at.

Get these rightAnything in the Digital or Tuning menus, because they're mode switches rather than amounts. SW 3 and SW 4 are different instruments, not neighbouring shades. Also the WAVE number, Waves Osc 60–63 where the analog waveforms live, EG1 Attack either side of 47, ENV2 → Loudness being above 00, and EG2 Release being open when you're listening for something after the key lifts.
Approximate freelyEnvelope times, LFO rate, cutoff, emphasis, and any mid-table wave position. If a lesson says EG1 Decay 40 and you land on 36, you'll hear the same thing. Turn the knob until it sounds right and treat the printed number as a description of the region.

The practical version: if a lesson doesn't sound the way it's described, check the mode switches before you check the knob values. A wrong SW or UW gives you a different sound entirely; a knob that's ten off just gives you a slightly different flavour of the right one.

The same goes for the global settings

The base patch uses FIRM 0, OSC 0 and LFO 0: original PPG behaviour throughout, including an LFO that drifts rather than holding a steady rate. That wobble is part of what the instrument sounds like, so it stays on by default. Switch LFO 1 on whenever you want a rate you can trust to stay put, and every lesson still works either way. Lesson 05 is the only lesson where the difference matters.

Holding a note while you change things

Play by hand for most of this. One hand holds a key and the other turns a knob. That's part of why knobs beat the keypad here: they work one-handed.

Two situations call for different approaches:

Static positionLesson 01, and Lesson 09's first experiment. Nothing moves on its own; you just want a continuous tone while you enter numbers. A held key is fine, and the options below free both hands.
Envelope-drivenLessons 02, 03, 06, 10 and 11. The envelope's shape is what you're listening to, so play the note yourself and let it run. A repeating sequence retriggers the envelope every step, so a slow attack never completes and you hear something quite unlike what the lesson describes.

A sustain pedal in the rear-panel socket is the best of both: one press triggers the envelope normally, the pedal holds the note at Envelope 2's sustain level, and both hands are free.

If you want both hands free

This only matters in Lesson 01. Three options:

Sustain pedalThe good one. Any footswitch in the rear-panel Sustain socket. One key press triggers the envelope normally and the note holds at Envelope 2's sustain level until you lift the pedal.
Tied sequencePress Record, play C3, then hold the key and press Rest/Tie repeatedly. Each press extends the note across one more step. Press Stop, set BPM 40 and DIV 0, then Play. Eight tied steps at 40 bpm gives about twelve seconds between retriggers.
Long MIDI noteSend a note-on from your DAW over USB and hold it. Without a pedal, this is the only way to hold one unbroken note.
GAT won't give you a drone

Gate time is a percentage of the step length and maxes out at 99, so there's always a gap and the envelope retriggers on every step. A single-step sequence at GAT 99 is a repeated note, not a held one. Tying across several steps is what makes the note long.

While a sequence runs, pressing any key transposes it, so stay off the keyboard. Press Stop before playing normally again. You don't need to touch SAVE: an unsaved sequence plays fine.

You should hear

A pure sine tone that starts and stops the instant you press and release a key. Wave 00 of wavetable 0 is an actual sine wave, with no harmonics and no grit. If you hear anything buzzy, something in the list above isn't set. This is your zero point.

If it isn't a sine

Re-dialling this patch between sessions is where leftovers from earlier lessons show up. Split what you're hearing in two, because the causes are different.

The sound changes while you hold one key. Something is modulating the wave position. In order of likelihood:

UW: the sweep switch

Learn why sweeps sound broken by default, and the one setting that fixes it.

The four analog waveforms at the end of the table are useful when you park on them and a problem when you sweep through them: a sweep that climbs the table hits saw, pulse, square, triangle in the last four slots and then wraps round to 00. Musically that's a lurch at the top of every sweep.

Stop the sequencer if it's running. From here on you're listening to the envelope's shape, and a repeating step would retrigger it. Change these five values:

Analog + Digital
ENV1 → Waves63 EG1 Attack · A140 EG1 Decay · D145 EG1 Sustain · S100 UW0

The attack value is the important one. The base patch has EG1 Attack at 00, which means the envelope is already at full the instant you press a key. The wave position jumps straight to the top of the table and you only hear it fall. An attack of 40 makes the climb audible.

If you only hear a downward sweep

EG1 Attack is still at 00. Every wave sweep travels up on the attack and back down on decay and release; with an instant attack the upward half happens faster than you can hear it. Any time a sweep seems to run the wrong way, that's the control to check.

Hold a note. Envelope 1 now runs the wave position up from 00 to the top of the table, then back down. Listen to the top of the sweep. Then set UW 2 and play the same note.

Now hear the wrap

Starting at 00 with a depth of 63 lands you exactly on 63, so nothing wraps. To hear wrapping, set UW 0 and Waves Osc 20: the envelope now aims for 83, which doesn't exist, so it rolls over the end of the table and reappears at the bottom. UW 2 costs you the analog waveforms

The trade is unavoidable: at UW 2 positions 60–63 are switched off, so you cannot park on the sawtooth, pulse, square or triangle. Any instruction to set a wave position of 60 or above only makes sense at UW 0.

Tested on firmware 1.0.11: the positions are clamped, not rescaled. At UW 2 the ceiling is 59, and entering 60, 61, 62 or 63 all sound identical to 59. The top of the knob's travel does nothing.

That has a consequence for sweeps. Waves Osc plus the ENV1 → Waves depth is the highest position the envelope aims for; if that total exceeds 59, the sweep arrives at the top before the envelope finishes rising and then holds there. An overshooting depth doesn't travel further. It travels faster and then sits still, which is why two patches with identical envelope times can seem to move at different speeds. For a sweep whose timing matches its envelope, keep Waves Osc plus depth at or below 59.

The same arithmetic tells you when UW 0 is safe: if the total stays well under 60, the sweep can never reach the wrap point, so you can keep the analog waveforms even in a patch that sweeps. Only a sweep that actually runs to the ceiling forces the trade.

UW does double duty

When a transient is loaded instead of a wavetable, UW stops being a table selector and becomes the loop length: 0 is 128 samples, 1 is 2048, 2 is 8192. Same parameter, entirely different meaning. Lesson 10 uses it that way.

03

Envelope 1 to Waves

The central move of this instrument: an envelope walking through the table. Learn how start point, depth and time interact.

Starting point
UW2 Waves Osc00 ENV1 → Waves63 EG1 Att · A125 EG1 Dec · D140 EG1 Sus · S100 EG1 Rel · R120

Attack 25 gives a climb you can hear. The envelope's shape is the path through the table: attack takes you up, decay brings you down to the sustain level, and release finishes the fall. Set attack to 00 at any point to hear how much of the journey you lose.

A. Depth

Move ENV1 → Waves through 00, 16, 32, 48, 63. Depth is how far up the table the envelope travels, measured in wave positions. Starting from Waves Osc 00 at UW 2, the ceiling is 59, so depths above about 59 stop adding distance and instead make the sweep finish early and hold at the top. This is a good one to sweep by knob and watch the number.

B. Start point

Put ENV1 → Waves back to 32, then move Waves Osc through 00, 12, 24, 36. Waves Osc is the floor the envelope climbs from and the envelope adds on top. Start at 24 with a depth of 32 and you travel 24 → 56.

C. Time

Set EG1 Decay (D1) to 10 (a percussive timbral snap), then 40 (a clear sweep), then 60 (a slow morph over seconds). This one control is the difference between a plucked digital bell and an evolving pad.

D. The runaway attack

Set EG1 Attack (A1) 50 and EG2 Release (R2) 50. Play a very short staccato note and take your hand off the keyboard: the sweep carries on without you. Any EG1 attack above 47 completes in full even if the key is released, then moves to its release phase. It's the trick behind the long PPG swells you trigger and walk away from.

Why EG2 Release matters here

Envelope 2 gates everything else. The base patch has EG2 Release at 00, so lifting the key shuts the amplifier instantly. The wave sweep still completes, but there's no sound left to carry it, so you hear nothing. Any effect that plays out after you release a key needs EG2 Release open enough to keep the voice alive through it.

Watch the OLED scope after you release. The waveform keeps changing shape while the tail fades, which is the sweep running on its own.

You should hear

Movement that is stepped rather than smooth: a staircase of timbres, not a filter opening. That granularity is the machine, not a fault. Lesson 04 has the switch that softens it.

Worth knowing

Envelope 1 drives both the filter and the wave position, with separate depth controls but a shared shape. To move them on different timings you need the third envelope, covered in the last section of Lesson 06.

Then try it on two other tables

Stay on 000 for all four experiments above. Changing the table while you're learning what depth, start point and time each do would move two variables at once. Before going on, though, run the same patch on 011 Chiaroscuro and 027 VoxForm.

Harmonic Bliss stacks its harmonics in strict order, so sweeping it sounds a lot like a filter opening. That makes it easy to follow, and slightly misleading about what wavetables are for. Chiaroscuro goes dark to bright and back within one sweep, a round trip no filter can make. VoxForm moves through vowel formants. That contrast is the actual lesson.

LFO and wheel to Waves

Cyclic timbral movement, and putting the table under your left hand.

Load WAVE 029, PulseShift Modulation. It runs from square through rectangular to narrow pulse, so an LFO walking through it is pulse-width modulation. Set UW 2, Waves Osc 20, ENV1 → Waves 00. Everything here happens at MW in the Digital menu.

The one lesson where the LFO global matters

The base patch runs LFO 0, the original, which drifts, audibly so at higher rates. That’s the authentic sound and it’s worth hearing. When you want to compare two rates or two shapes and be sure the difference is the thing you changed, switch to LFO 1 in the User menu. A. Fixed cyclic movement

Set MW 6, LFO Rate 12, Waveshape 00. A slow triangle walks the table up and down, making a pad that never settles. Now push LFO Rate to 50: the same route travelled fast reads as a buzzing, unstable timbre rather than movement.

B. The four LFO shapes

Keep MW 6 and LFO Rate near 12. The four shapes sit across the 00–63 range of the Waveshape control in this order: triangle, ramp, sawtooth, square. Turn the knob slowly through the whole range and watch the number.

Verify by ear

The manual gives the order but not the switching points. Square is the giveaway: it alternates hard between two timbres with nothing in between. Find where it starts and you've mapped the boundaries. Write them in the margin.

C. The wheel as a scanner

Set MW 2 and hold a chord while moving the wheel slowly. This is the most expressive control on the instrument and the reason to play it rather than sequence it. The wheel's position is stored in the patch, separately for Group A and B.

D. Delay, and which routes it reaches

Set MW 1 and push the mod wheel fully up before playing, then set LFO Delay 30. Modulation fades in a few seconds after the note; at 63 the delay is ten seconds. This is how pads start still and come alive as they're held.

Now set MW 6 and play again with the same Delay value. The modulation arrives instantly. Nothing is broken:

The rule

LFO Delay only reaches mod-wheel-scaled routes. MW 1, MF 1 and MO/MS in the Tuning menu all take the delay. The fixed-depth settings (MW 3–9, MF 4–9, ML 1) bypass it and modulate from the first instant. The wheel-scaled paths run through a common amount stage where the delay ramp lives; the fixed-depth ones tap the LFO after it.

So a pad that swells into movement has to use MW 1 with the wheel raised, not MW 3–9. You give up setting depth as a number and gain both the delay and a live performance control over how much movement the sound has. For a pad that's a fair trade. Remember the wheel's position is stored per patch, per group.

Scope of this finding

Established by testing four routes (MW 6, MW 1, MF 6 and ML 1) one at a time on firmware 1.0.11, with FIRM 0 and LFO 1. It isn't documented in the manual. Note what was not tested: the LFO and FIRM settings were never varied, so this says nothing about whether they affect the delay. The base patch now runs LFO 0, so if the wheel-scaled route delays for you as described, the finding holds there too; if a fixed-depth route delays at LFO 0, that's new information and the rule is narrower than stated.

E. Up and down, in rhythm

Everything so far sweeps up the table and falls back. There are three ways to make the wave position rise and fall repeatedly, and the choice comes down to whether it should lock to tempo.

Free-running: the LFO

Set MW 6, LFO Waveshape 00 (triangle), LFO Rate 20, with UW 2 and Waves Osc 10 so it has room to travel. A triangle goes up, then down, forever.

Now turn the Waveshape to the top of its range for the square. Instead of gliding, the timbre snaps between two positions. It sounds far more rhythmic, and it's a classic of the instrument.

The limitation: the LFO is not synced to the sequencer or to anything else. You can tune the rate by ear to sit near a tempo, but it will slowly drift against the beat. Good for texture, less good for anything that has to lock to a drum part.

Locked to tempo: the sequencer retriggers Envelope 1

Every sequencer step retriggers the envelopes. The behaviour that made the sequencer useless as a drone earlier is exactly what you want here. Set MW 0, ENV1 → Waves 40, EG1 (A1 D1 S1 R1) 12 / 20 / 00 / 15, keeping UW 2 and Waves Osc 10. Record eight steps of the same note and press Play.

Each step now sweeps up on the attack and back down on the decay, landing exactly on the grid. BPM and DIV set the rhythm; EG1's attack and decay set the shape inside each step. Keep the two short enough to finish within a step, or consecutive sweeps blur into each other. At 110 bpm in sixteenths a step is only around 135 ms.

Following the notes: arpeggiator plus keyboard tracking

Set ENV1 → Waves 00, KW 3 so higher notes sit higher in the table, then in the Sequencer menu set MOD 13: arpeggiator, up and down. Hold a chord spread over two octaves. The arpeggio climbs and falls in pitch, and the wave position climbs and falls with it, in time.

Stacking them

The sequencer method combines well with SW 2 from Lesson 06: Envelope 1 sweeps the main oscillator on every step while Envelope 3, with its own attack and decay, sweeps the sub. Two movements per note, both on the grid.

06

The second oscillator

SW changes the Wave more than any other parameter: it turns the second oscillator into seven different instruments. Rather than counting from 0 to 6, follow one question through the lesson: what does the WAVES-SUB knob mean in this mode?

The "sub" isn't a sub. It's a full second oscillator with its own position in the same wavetable, and depending on SW it can be a partner, a modulator, a noise source, or nothing at all. WAVES-SUB is the knob that changes meaning most dramatically between them.

Start from WAVE 000, UW 2, Waves Osc 20, SW 3. That's the base patch with the second oscillator switched off: one waveform, your reference point for everything below.

That's the order this lesson takes them in. SW 2 is last because it doesn't change the oscillator at all. It changes which envelope does the sweeping.

SW 1: a second, independent voice

Set SW 1 and Waves Sub 55. You now have two oscillators sitting at different points in the same table: the main at position 20, the sub high up where the harmonics are dense.

Why 55 and not 60? This lesson keeps UW 2, which switches off positions 60–63, and step 5 below sweeps the main oscillator to the top of the table, which is exactly what UW 2 is for. You cannot have the analog waveforms and a no-wrap sweep at the same time. Lesson 07 makes the opposite trade.

  1. Turn Waves Sub slowly from 00 to 63 with the main oscillator fixed. You're mixing two points of one wavetable, and the combinations are not intuitive: a bright sub under a dull main sounds nothing like the reverse.
  2. Now leave the sub at 55 and turn Waves Osc instead. Same two voices, opposite roles. Notice which combinations sound like one thick instrument and which sound like two separate ones.
  3. Add DETU 1, then 2. Two oscillators at slightly different pitches is where width comes from. This is the biggest jump in size you'll hear all lesson.
  4. Try DETU 6 (octave up) and DETU 8 (octave down). With Waves Sub at 60 and DETU 8 you get a real sub-bass underneath a moving top voice.
  5. Put DETU back to 2, then add ENV1 → Waves 44 with EG1 25 / 40 / 00 / 20. The main oscillator sweeps; the sub stays exactly where you left it.
Why this is the pad recipe

A moving voice over a stationary one gives you movement and an anchor at the same time. All the motion is in the main oscillator while the sub holds the sound's identity in place. Sweep with nothing anchoring it and a pad tends to drift into unrecognisability.

SW 0: the sub follows, at a fixed distance

Keep the sweep running and switch to SW 0 with Waves Sub 00. Now the sub tracks the main oscillator's position, and WAVES-SUB sets how far behind or ahead it sits.

  1. At Waves Sub 00 both oscillators are at the same position, doubling the same waveform. Compare to SW 3 and listen for the thickening.
  2. Turn Waves Sub to 04, then 10, then 20. The two now sweep in parallel, separated by that many table positions. Small offsets read as chorus; large ones as two instruments moving in formation.
  3. A/B against SW 1 a few times with the sweep running. The difference is the whole point: at SW 0 both voices move, at SW 1 only one does.
  4. Add DETU 2 on top. Parallel sweeping plus detuning is the classic big PPG sweep.
A subtlety to listen for

With UW 2 the offset can push the sub against the ceiling of the table while the main oscillator is still climbing, so the gap between them closes at the top of a sweep. It's not a fault, and it's one reason large offsets sound less uniform than small ones.

SW 6: noise, where Waves-Sub becomes a level

Set SW 6 and put ENV1 → Waves back to 00. The second oscillator is now a noise generator and WAVES-SUB is now the noise level instead of a position.

  1. Waves Sub 10: breath behind the tone. 30: clearly present. 63: noise dominates.
  2. At Waves Sub 30, bring VCF Cutoff down from 63 to about 30. Unfiltered noise is hiss; filtered noise is air, and it's the difference between a pad sounding cheap and sounding breathy.
  3. Add Emphasis 40 and sweep cutoff by hand. Resonant filtered noise is a wind or surf sound in its own right.
  4. For percussion: Waves Sub 63, EG2 00 / 15 / 00 / 10, Cutoff 50. That's a hi-hat. Lower the cutoff and lengthen the decay for a snare-ish noise burst.

This is the instrument's only noise source, so it's also your only route to wind, breath, surf, and unpitched percussion.

SW 5: ring modulation

Set SW 5 and EG2 back to 00 / 63 / 63 / 00. The sub is no longer heard directly. Its output multiplies with the main oscillator, producing sum and difference frequencies that mostly aren't harmonics of either. WAVES-SUB picks which waveform does the modulating.

  1. Start with Waves Sub 00: a sine, the simplest modulator, giving the cleanest bell tones. Then turn it slowly upward. Richer modulators produce denser, more clangorous results; by 60 it's close to noise.
  2. Now the important control: DETU. At 0 the two oscillators are in tune, the sums and differences land on harmonics, and it reads as a timbre change. At 1 and 3 it becomes inharmonic and metallic. At 5, 6 and 7 you get fixed-interval ring mod, which is stable enough to play melodically.
  3. Load WAVE 009 VoxEnsemble and repeat. Its strong mid formants are the ones the manual singles out for ring modulation, and the difference is obvious.
  4. Add a percussive envelope: EG2 00 / 30 / 00 / 25, Cutoff 35, ENV1 → VCF 30. That's a lo-fi electric bell.
Where this fits your work

Ring mod with slight detuning is one of the few ways to get inharmonic content out of this instrument, which is exactly what makes a bell or a struck metal sound convincing. Combined with OSC 0's aliasing it lands squarely in lo-fi territory without a plugin.

SW 4: hard sync, where Waves-Sub stops being a wave at all

Set SW 4, DETU 0, Waves Osc 60, and EG2 back to 00 / 63 / 63 / 00. The sub is muted and its only job is to reset the main oscillator every cycle. WAVES-SUB now sets the main oscillator's frequency, the biggest change of meaning in any mode.

  1. Hold a note and turn Waves Sub slowly upward from 00. The pitch you hear stays fixed while the timbre tears through a series of formants. This is the sound sync exists for, and it's much better turned by hand than typed.
  2. Park on 08, 14, 22, 30 and 40 in turn. Each is a distinct vowel-like character rather than a point on a gradient.
  3. Change Waves Osc between 60 (saw) and 20. The waveform being reset still matters: a sawtooth gives the hardest tear, gentler waves a softer one.
  4. Play the same setting at the bottom and top of the keyboard. Because the sync frequency is fixed rather than tracking pitch, the character shifts across the range.
  5. For a lead: KEYB 3 (unison), Cutoff 45, Emphasis 22, ENV1 → VCF 18, EG1 00 / 30 / 20 / 20. Then set MF 2 to put cutoff on the mod wheel and play it properly.

SW 2: two oscillators, two sweep envelopes

The odd one out, and the most capable setting here. SW 2 doesn't change what the second oscillator is. It stays a normal wavetable voice. What changes is which envelope sweeps it: Envelope 3 takes over the sub oscillator's wave position, while Envelope 1 carries on sweeping the main oscillator exactly as it always does.

So this is the one setting where the two oscillators can travel through the table on completely independent timings. Everywhere else they either move together (SW 0), or one moves while the other sits still (SW 1).

What the filter is for

Understand the division of labour: the digital section chooses harmonics, the analog section removes them.

A 24 dB/octave low-pass per voice, based on the SSM2044. It's the reason the Wave sounds warm rather than merely digital.

Set UW 0 and Waves Osc 60, the analog sawtooth, which gives the filter the maximum number of harmonics to work on. The UW value matters here: at UW 2 positions 60–63 are switched off, so you would not be hearing a sawtooth at all. Nothing in this lesson sweeps the wave position, so wrapping is irrelevant and UW 0 is the right choice.

A. Cutoff and the resonance penalty

Set VCF Cutoff 32. Raise VCF Emphasis through 00, 20, 40, 55, 63.

You should hear

The resonant peak sharpens and the overall output level drops as emphasis rises. That level loss is a characteristic of the SSM2044, not a fault. Below about half way you get the classic bright sound; push far enough and the filter self-oscillates into a sine at the cutoff frequency.

Getting the level back

ENV2 → Loudness is the obvious answer and it works, but only if you left yourself room. The base patch has it at 63, so there is nothing above. It's really a balance control, setting the active group's output level so Group A and Group B can be matched, rather than a makeup gain.

So the fix is to plan for it. Build patches with ENV2 → Loudness around 45–50 and you have headroom to compensate when resonance costs you level, and your patches sit at comparable volumes when you switch between them. The base patch uses 63 because these lessons want the raw waveform at full level, which is right for listening and wrong for building a library.

Failing that, Master Volume fixes what you are hearing right now, and the M4's input gain fixes what you are recording. Neither is stored in the patch, so neither travels with it.

Turn the sub off first

Start with SW 3. With both oscillators running they play the same sample from two start points, and moving one while the other stays put is very hard to hear, because the unchanged voice masks the change. Almost everything below is inaudible until you isolate a single oscillator. Bring the sub back in section D, deliberately.

Enter 036 at WAVE in the Program menu. That's Xylophon, a hard-mallet sample whose sharp attack makes changes easy to hear. Set SW 3, UW 0, Waves Osc 00, ENV1 → Waves 00.

Open the loop point before anything else

At ENV1 → Waves 00 the loop sits at the very start of the sample, so playback reaches it almost immediately and repeats a sliver only a few milliseconds long. Practically none of the recording is being played, and on transient 036 the start point has no audible effect at all in this state: Waves Osc does nothing until the loop point is opened up. Whatever the mechanism, treat ENV1 → Waves at 00 as a dead position rather than a neutral one. This is the reverse of the wavetable lessons, where 00 is exactly the sensible place to start.

A. The loop point, first

Move ENV1 → Waves through 04, 10, 16, 22. This sets how far playback gets before it reaches the loop and starts repeating, so it controls both how much of the sample you hear and which fragment is held for as long as the key is down.

At 04 you get a short blip and a held tone. By 22 most of the mallet hit plays out before it settles. Somewhere around 16–20 is a good working value for the rest of this lesson.

The ceiling is different for every transient

On 036, values from about 26 upward produce silence: the loop point has landed past the end of the sample data, so there is nothing left to repeat. That ceiling is a property of the individual sample's length rather than a fixed number. A long choir transient will take a much higher value than a short mallet hit. When a transient goes quiet as you raise this control, you have run off the end rather than broken something.

B. The start point

Leave ENV1 → Waves at 20 so there is something to hear, then move Waves Osc through 00, 06, 12, 18. You are skipping further into the sample each time, so the mallet attack thins and then disappears, leaving you in the body of the note.

This is the control that turns one sample into several instruments. The attack of a percussive sample carries most of its identity; remove it and what remains is often unrecognisable as the same source.

Note that the two controls interact: the start point moves where playback begins and the loop point is a fixed distance into the sample, so raising Waves Osc also shortens the stretch you hear before the loop arrives. Push it far enough and you reach the loop immediately, which sounds much like the ENV1 → Waves 00 case you started from.

Why a short loop sounds electronic

Every wavetable on the Wave is 64 waveforms of 128 samples each, and a UW 0 loop is also 128 samples. The shortest loop is therefore repeating exactly one waveform's worth of audio through the same playback engine, which is why it stops sounding like a sample and starts sounding like an oscillator, with its pitch tracking the keyboard normally.

The crunch is the loop seam. An arbitrary slice of a recording almost never begins and ends at the same point in its cycle, so there is a discontinuity every 128 samples. It's the same artefact that makes OSC 0 gritty between adjacent waveforms, except here it repeats every cycle instead of once per transition.

What this does not mean is that ENV1 → Waves works like a wavetable position control. In use it reads as a length control: low values give electronic buzz because almost nothing plays before the loop arrives, and raising it lets progressively more of the recording through until you hear the instrument the sample came from. The looped content changes too, but that is not the effect you notice.

Then go back and move ENV1 → Waves again at UW 2. It does much less than it did at UW 0. With a loop this long, shifting where it starts still leaves you hearing most of the same material, and on a short sample the loop may cover nearly all of it. The loop point control is at its most useful with short loops.

Scope of the above

Established by ear on transient 036 at firmware 1.0.11. The interaction between loop point and loop length will differ with sample length, so treat the specific numbers as belonging to this transient rather than as general rules. What should hold everywhere: short loops make the loop point matter, long loops flatten its effect, and both run out of sample eventually.

D. Now bring the sub back

Set SW 1. Both oscillators now play the sample from independent start points, and this is where moving them separately becomes worth doing.

  1. With Waves Osc 00, move Waves Sub to 06, then 12, then 20. Two copies of the same sample at a small offset make a doubling that gets progressively looser.
  2. Add DETU 1. Offset in both time and pitch, which is close to a granular smear.
  3. Move both controls together, keeping the gap constant, and the pair travels through the sample as a unit.

If any of this stops being audible, go back to SW 3 and check the control is doing what you think before adding the second voice.

Also worth a listen: 046 Cath. Choir and 047 ChoirVerse at UW 2 with a slow EG2 attack; 039 Soft Bell EP and 040 Medium EP at UW 0 for lo-fi keys.

Why this matters for lo-fi

Two different instruments live here. At UW 2, with the loop point well open, a transient is a one-shot sampler with an analog filter after it. At UW 0 with a low loop point it is closer to a rough oscillator built from a fragment of a recording: harsher, more synthetic, and the more distinctive of the two. Combined with OSC 0's aliasing it degrades in a way that is hard to fake with plugins. You can load your own mono WAVs into slots 049–063 through SynthTribe at 8 or 16 bit, with no downsampling applied to transients. Those slots are empty from the factory, so nothing gets overwritten.

Envelope 2 and the shape of a note

The loudness envelope decides whether something is a pluck, a pad or a swell, and gates whether you hear anything else at all. Everything up to now has deliberately used an organ-like setting so the raw waveform came through cleanly.

Set Waves Osc 20 and leave the rest of the base patch alone. All four EG2 knobs are in the ADSR-ENVELOPE 2 section.

A. One stage at a time

  1. Start at 00 / 63 / 63 / 00, the base patch. Instant on, instant off, like an organ.
  2. EG2 Attack 40. Now the note fades in. Play staccato and you barely get any sound; the attack never completes.
  3. Attack back to 00, Decay 20, Sustain 00. A pluck. The note dies while you're still holding the key, which is what decay-to-zero means.
  4. Sustain 30. The decay now falls to a level and holds there. Sustain is the only one of the four that's a level rather than a time.
  5. Release 45. The note now rings on after you let go. Compare against Release 00 a few times. This is the difference between an instrument that sounds sampled and one that sounds played.

Envelope 1, one shape and two jobs

Envelope 1 drives the filter and the wave position at the same time, from a single shape, with separate depth knobs. Balancing those two is most of what programming this instrument comes down to.

Set Waves Osc 08, VCF Cutoff 20, VCF Emphasis 30, EG1 00 / 30 / 12 / 25, and both depth knobs to 00.

A. Filter only

Set ENV1 → VCF 45. A conventional subtractive pluck: the filter opens on the attack and closes as the envelope falls. Vary EG1 Decay from 10 to 50 and hear the whole range from a click to a slow bloom. Nothing digital is happening: this is the Wave as an analog polysynth.

B. Wave position only

Set ENV1 → VCF 00 and ENV1 → Waves 45. Same envelope, same timing, different destination. The brightness now changes because different harmonics are being generated, not because a filter is letting more through.

The difference to listen for

A filter sweep is smooth and its character is uniform: the same sound, more or less of it. A wave sweep changes character as it goes, with steps and sometimes a change in perceived loudness. Play them back to back until you can tell them apart blind. Telling them apart is the most useful ear skill on this instrument.

C. Both together

Set both depths to 30. They reinforce: the table supplies harmonics while the filter simultaneously lets more through. This is the default way most Wave patches are built, and it's why they open up so convincingly.

Now try them in opposition: ENV1 → Waves 50 with Cutoff 55 and ENV1 → VCF 00. The wave sweep adds harmonics while the filter stays put, which is a flatter but more clearly digital character.

D. The limitation, and what to do about it

One shape drives both. You cannot have a fast filter attack and a slow wave bloom from Envelope 1 alone. The depths are independent but the timing isn't. That's exactly the gap Envelope 3 fills, via SW 2. Lesson 14 covers it.

E. Velocity to the filter

Set VF to 2. Now how hard you play sets how far the filter opens. Combined with VL 2 from the last lesson, the patch responds to touch in both brightness and volume, which is most of what makes a synth patch feel playable.

F. The runaway attack, again

Set EG1 Attack 50 with EG2 Release 45. Above 47 the attack completes whether you hold the key or not. On the filter this gives you a swell you trigger and release; on the wave position it gives the long sweeps from Lesson 03.

Envelope 3, the second gesture

The only envelope with no default destination. It does nothing until you route it, and there are exactly two places it can go: oscillator pitch, or wave position.

It's also the only AD envelope (attack and decay, no sustain and no release), so it rises once and falls back regardless of how long you hold the key. In place of the missing stages it has ENV 3 ATT, an attenuation control that sets how far it travels.

Set Waves Osc 20 and EG3 00 / 15 / 32.

A. Pitch, upward and downward

Set EO 1 in the Tuning menu. That routes Envelope 3 to the main oscillator's pitch. Nothing happens yet, because attenuation at 32 is the centre, the neutral point.

  1. Raise EG3 Attenuation to 45. Each note now starts above pitch and falls into place. At 60 it's a dramatic drop.
  2. Lower it to 20, then 05. Below centre the modulation inverts: notes now swoop up into pitch from below.
  3. With Attenuation 55 and EG3 Decay 08, you have a percussive pitch blip, the basis of synthetic toms and kicks.
  4. Lengthen EG3 Decay to 40 for a slow drift into tune, which is a convincing tape-warble effect and very much at home in lo-fi.

B. Portamento

Set EO to 2, then work up to 9, playing legato lines. Values above 1 stop being an envelope and become glide, with increasing time. Combine with KEYB 3 (unison) for a classic mono lead that slides between notes.

C. The sub oscillator only

Set EO 0 and ES 1, with SW 1 and Waves Sub 60. Now only the second oscillator's pitch moves. Each note starts detuned and settles, which is a much livelier version of what DETU does statically.

D. The sub oscillator's wave position

The other destination, covered in full in Lesson 06. Setting SW 2 hands Envelope 3 the sub oscillator's position in the wavetable, with ENV 3 ATT as its depth and WAVES-SUB as its starting point. Envelope 1 keeps sweeping the main oscillator, so the two voices move independently.

Set EO 0, ES 0, SW 2, DETU 6, Waves Osc 08, Waves Sub 08, ENV1 → Waves 44, EG1 00 / 18 / 00 / 20, EG3 45 / 30 / 55, EG2 Release 45.

You should hear

Two sweeps an octave apart on different clocks: the main oscillator moving fast and falling back, the sub blooming slowly behind it and continuing past the key release. Set DETU to 0 and the same two movements collapse into one voice whose harmonics travel in two directions at once.

Attenuation changes meaning with destination

On pitch, the centre of the range is neutral, below is negative, above is positive. On wave position the manual describes it running from no modulation at minimum to the full 63 positions at maximum, added to the Waves Sub value. Sweep the knob with a note held and confirm which behaviour you're getting before you build a patch on it.

Recording and editing a sequence

Up to 64 steps with up to eight notes on each, stored inside the program. Notes go in one at a time and the clock decides when they happen. You're entering what, never when.

Any patch works, but steps are easiest to hear on something percussive. Use the Pluck setting from Lesson 12: Waves Osc 20, EG2 00 / 22 / 00 / 18.

The four buttons under the display are Rest/Tie, Record, Stop and Play/Pause. Holding Shift gives each a second function (Settings, Append, Clr Lst and Restart), and that's where most of the editing lives.

A. Put something in

  1. Press Record.
  2. Play four notes, one at a time. Each keypress becomes one step, however long you hold it.
  3. Press Stop, then Play.

Chords work the same way: press three keys together and that's one step containing three notes. Eight is the ceiling per step.

B. Rests are the rhythm

Press Record again and keep going. With no key held, press Rest/Tie to enter a silent step.

Record eight steps as note, rest, note, rest, note, note, rest, rest. Then record eight consecutive notes and compare. The second is a scale exercise; the first is a part. On a step sequencer, where you don't put notes does more work than where you do.

C. Ties make long notes

Hold a note and press Rest/Tie. That extends it across an extra step. Press it repeatedly for longer notes.

This is how you get mixed note lengths out of a fixed grid: set DIV to sixteenths for the fast material, then tie the notes that need to be long. A bassline of two tied quarter notes and eight sixteenths is one sequence, not two.

D. Navigating and fixing

Shift + < >Step through what you've recorded. Each step sounds as you land on it, so you can hear where you are.
Shift + StopClr Lst: erases the last step recorded. Press repeatedly to walk backwards through mistakes.
Shift + Stop, heldMore than two seconds erases the entire sequence.
STEPIn the Sequencer menu, shows the current step and the total recorded. It's the only place you can see how long your sequence is.

E. Overdub

Set the first digit of OVP to 1 in the Sequencer menu. Re-entering Record now adds notes to existing steps instead of replacing them.

The practical use: record a single-note line first and get the rhythm right, then overdub a second pass adding the octave below on the strong steps only. That's much easier than playing both at once, and it's how you build something denser than your hands can enter in one go.

F. Append

Shift + Record enters Append, shown at bottom right of the display. Hold Shift and use < and > to move to a step, then play.

At step 00New steps go in before the current first step.
At the last stepNew steps extend the sequence, up to 64.
Anywhere betweenNew steps are inserted after the one you navigated to.

Append inserts steps; overdub adds notes to a step. They're two different operations that both look like "record more."

Timing and feel

Three controls decide whether a sequence sounds like synthwave, lo-fi or ambient, and none of them touch the notes.

Keep the sequence from Lesson 15 running. Everything here is in the Sequencer menu, under Shift + Settings.

A. The three controls

ControlRangeWhat it changes
BPM40–240Tempo. Nothing else.

B. BPM and DIV are not the same knob

Play your sequence at BPM 120, DIV 1. Now try BPM 240, DIV 0. The notes arrive at the same rate, but they're not equivalent: the second one is at a tempo twice as fast, so if you later sync to a DAW or clock the Crave from this, everything downstream is at the wrong tempo.

Set the BPM to the tempo of the music, then use DIV to decide what the sequence plays against it. Getting into that habit now saves confusion the moment there's a second device in the picture.

C. Gate is the feel control

At DIV 2 and BPM 112, run through GAT 15, 35, 60, 90.

You should hear

At 15 the notes are clipped and percussive, because the envelope barely gets going. At 90 they nearly touch, which on a patch with any release becomes a continuous line. Same notes, same tempo: this control alone is the difference between a stabbing arpeggio and a pad.

Gate interacts with EG2. A long gate on a patch with a short decay still sounds staccato, because the envelope has already finished. If GAT changes aren't doing much, look at EG2 Decay and Sustain.

The sequencer doesn't know about bars. It loops however many steps you recorded. Record seven steps at DIV 2 and let it run against a four-beat count. The pattern rotates against the pulse and takes seven bars to come back around.

That's rhythmic interest for no extra work, and it's especially useful for ambient material where you want something that repeats but never quite lands the same way. Five, seven, eleven and thirteen all do it.

Playing the sequencer

The keyboard stays live while a sequence runs, and what it does is switchable. This is what turns an eight-step loop into a performance.

A. The two modes

The second digit of OVP decides what the keyboard does during playback.

x0Transpose. Any key you press instantly moves the whole sequence. Default.
x1Independent. The keyboard plays normally, using whatever voices the sequencer isn't. Solo over your own part.

OVP's first digit is overdub and the second is this: two unrelated settings sharing one display field.

B. Progressions from one riff

Set OVP x0, put on the Night Drive settings from the projects section if you have them, and record any eight-step pattern in A minor. Now play, holding each root for one bar:

Left hand, one bar each
Bar 1A Bar 2F Bar 3C Bar 4G

One recorded pattern, four bars of music. The riff keeps its internal shape and moves as a whole, which is how this genre is built.

Transposition is instant rather than quantised, so land your changes on the beat yourself. Practise it a few times before recording.

C. Velocity across the whole pattern

With VL set to 2 or 3 in the Digital menu, the velocity of the key you transpose with applies to every step for as long as you hold it. Press softly for a quiet bar and hard for an accent. You get dynamics across a sequence you never played dynamically.

D. Soloing over the top

Set OVP x1. The sequencer takes the voices it needs and leaves the rest. A single-note sequence leaves seven voices free; an eight-note chord sequence leaves none, and the keyboard will go silent. Keep the recorded part thin if you intend to play over it.

E. Transport as performance

Play/PauseA second press pauses where it is; a third resumes from that point. Unlike Stop, it doesn't reset to step one.
StopStops and resets to the first step.
Shift + PlayRestart: jumps back to step one while running. Use it to force a pattern back into line after a transposition lands late.

Pausing mid-pattern and resuming somewhere unexpected is a legitimate arrangement move, particularly on odd step counts where the sequence is already floating against the pulse.

F. Two sounds at once

Set KEYB 1 (Quad) or one of the split modes and the sequence plays through both Group A and Group B, so one part can be two layered sounds. In a split mode, the sequencer and your left hand can be on entirely different patches.

The same clock and gate controls, but the notes come from whatever you're holding rather than from a recorded pattern. Better than the sequencer whenever the harmony needs to change faster than you can re-record.

Saving, syncing and driving the rig

Sequences live inside programs, and the Wave's clock can either follow your DAW or run everything else in the room.

A. Saving a sequence

In the Sequencer menu, type 1 against SAVE. That stores the sequence together with its GAT and DIV values into the current program.

Notice that a program holds a sound and a part, so changing program changes both. That turns program slots into song sections rather than just patches: one for the verse bassline, one for the chorus, and switching between them becomes the arrangement.

SAVE stores the sequence, but the sound still needs the usual DTF → 9 write. They're two separate operations.

B. Copying a sequence between programs

  1. Load the program you want to copy to.
  2. In the Sequencer menu, move the cursor to PR.
  3. Hold Shift and type the number of the program you want to copy from.
  4. Release Shift. The sequence copies across.

This is how you get one part played by several sounds: copy the bassline into three programs and give each a different patch, then switch between them mid-take.

C. Clock source

CLK takes two digits: source, then rate.

0Internal: the Wave is the master.
1DIN MIDI in.
2USB MIDI in. Use this for GarageBand or Logic over the USB cable.
3Analog trigger via the rear-panel Sync In.

The second digit sets the rate for the internal clock and analog trigger only: 0 one pulse per step, 1 2 ppqn, 2 24 ppqn, 3 48 ppqn. Both MIDI sources are fixed at 24 ppqn, and the second digit is ignored when either is selected.

These settings govern what the Wave follows. Going the other way, the Wave transmits MIDI clock on its MIDI Out regardless. That was confirmed by ear, since the manual's implementation table lists clock as received but not as transmitted.

D. Following the DAW

Set CLK 2 and press play in your DAW. The Wave's sequencer follows the transport, and the BPM field shows the incoming tempo rather than letting you set it.

This is the setup for building a track around a Wave part: get the sequence right by ear on the internal clock, then switch to CLK 2 so takes drop straight onto the grid.

E. Driving the Crave and the Edge

Sync Out is analog sync, not MIDI. It carries a clock tick on the tip and a start/stop gate on the ring: voltage pulses, with no note or channel information. The Korg Volcas and SQ-1 use the same scheme.

Why analog rather than MIDI here

MIDI is a data stream and the notes travel with the clock whether you want them or not, so a MIDI-synced Crave ends up playing the Wave's sequence instead of its own. Putting the two boxes on different MIDI channels fixes that, since clock is channel-independent while notes are not. Analog sync sidesteps it entirely: there is nothing on the wire except a clock and a transport gate.

It also gives you back control of the rate. Under MIDI clock the Crave runs at sixteenth notes with no way to divide it down, and the Edge's SCALE control is the only lever. Under analog pulses the Wave decides.

Wiring all three

The Wave is the natural master: its sequencer is the only one of the three holding 64 steps and eight notes per step, so it carries harmonic material the others cannot. Everything then runs into the mixer and out to the M4, with no computer involved.

Both boxes want the same pair of signals, so you wire the same pattern twice.

SignalFromCraveEdge

Set each box to listen for it: on the Crave, the clock source lives in settings mode under Shift + HOLD/REST + 8. On the Edge, set the CLOCK switch to INT/EXT. The manual specifies the ADV/CLOCK patch point as the external clocking route, and each rising edge advances the sequencer one step.

What to buy

The connectors do not match. The Wave's Sync In and Sync Out are ¼" TRS, in keeping with every other jack on the instrument, while analog sync on the Crave, the Edge and most other gear is 3.5 mm. And one Sync Out has to reach four jacks.

  • 1 × ¼" TRS male to two 3.5 mm TS male breakout, sometimes sold as a kind of insert cable. This splits tip from ring. A ¼" TRS insert cable into two ¼"-to-3.5 mm adapters does the same job with more connectors in the path.
  • 2 × passive 3.5 mm Y-splitters, one on the clock line and one on start/stop.
  • 4 × short 3.5 mm TS patch cables.

Splitting a pulse passively into two high-impedance CV inputs is fine, because neither box loads it meaningfully. Splitting at the source is also the only option, since neither the Crave nor the Edge has a clock output to daisy-chain from.

Setting the rate

Set the second digit of CLK to 0 (one pulse per step), and all three machines advance together, one step each per pulse. Confirm it by ear before building anything on top: one Wave step should move the Crave and the Edge by exactly one.

From there the Wave's DIV decides what a step means musically, and it applies to the whole rig at once. Both boxes' own tempo controls go inert while they are following an external clock. That's the point: one tempo, one transport, three instruments.

If you would rather use MIDI

The Wave does transmit MIDI clock, so Wave MIDI Out into the Crave's MIDI In works, with the Crave set to MIDI clock in the same settings mode. The Crave's OUT/THRU jack set to THRU then passes the stream along to the Edge. Expect the note-bleed problem unless the boxes are on separate MIDI channels, and expect to lose the rate control that DIV gives you.

Three sections to build

Complete builds in synthwave, lo-fi and ambient, each with a patch, a sequence and a way to perform it. Each one uses most of what's in this workbook, and each is meant to end up as a recorded take rather than a saved preset.

How to work these

Build the patch, record the sequence, then play the result. The transposition and mod wheel moves are what turn a loop into a section. Record straight into the M4 as you go; the takes where you were still figuring out the wheel movement are usually the good ones. Each project shows its full state under the Settings button.

Two pieces to play

Whole pieces of music for one person and one instrument: the sequencer holds down one part while you play another over it, each with its own sound. Both use split keyboard mode, which is what makes two simultaneous patches possible.

How the split works

A program contains two sounds, Group A and Group B. In keyboard modes 4 through 8 the split point divides them: Group A on the upper keyboard, Group B on the lower. Record the sequence from the lower half and it plays through Group B; your right hand above the split plays Group A.

  1. Set KEYB and SPLT in the Program menu. SPLT is a note number where the lowest octave in –2 mode is 0–12, –1 mode is 13–24, 0 mode is 25–36 and so on. The note you choose becomes the lowest note of the upper keyboard.
  2. Press the Group button so only B is lit, and dial the accompaniment patch.
  3. Switch to A and dial the lead patch.
  4. Record the sequence playing below the split point.
  5. Set OVP's second digit to 1 so your playing doesn't transpose the sequence.
Worth confirming by ear

The manual documents the split as upper/lower for the two groups but doesn't state explicitly which group the sequencer plays through. The behaviour above, with recorded notes below the split sounding through Group B, is what the split logic implies. Record two notes, one either side of the split, and listen before building on it. If it doesn't hold, use KEYB 0 with one sound for both parts and OVP x1, which works regardless.

The 200 factory programs

Two banks of 100. Bank 0 is Behringer’s own material, Bank 1 recreates classic Wave programs. Useful mainly as raw material for taking apart.

Reverse-engineering a preset is the fastest way to consolidate everything in this workbook: load one, write down every value from the Digital, Tuning and Analog menus, then rebuild it from the base patch until they match. Three of those teach more than another dozen lessons.

Abbreviations & fixes

Abbreviations

Everything the display and panel shorten, in one place.

Synthesis terms

EGEnvelope generator. EG1, EG2, EG3 are the three envelopes and their A/D/S/R knobs.
ENVThe same word, used on the three depth knobs in Modifiers Control: ENV 1 ▸ VCF, ENV 2 ▸ LOUDNESS, ENV 1 ▸ WAVES.
ADSRAttack, Decay, Sustain, Release: the four stages of Envelopes 1 and 2. Sustain is a level; the other three are times.
ADAttack and Decay only, with no sustain level and no release. Envelope 3, which rises once and falls back regardless of how long you hold the key.
ATTAttenuation: how far Envelope 3 travels, in place of the sustain and release it doesn't have.
LFOLow frequency oscillator. One shared across all eight voices, 0.2–12 Hz, used for modulation rather than as a sound source.
VCFVoltage-controlled filter. Analog, 24 dB per octave low-pass, one per voice, based on the SSM2044.
VCAVoltage-controlled amplifier: the loudness stage, driven by Envelope 2.
DACDigital-to-analog converter, between the oscillators and the filter.
WT / TRWavetable and transient. The display shows WAVE for a wavetable and TRAN for a transient.
Group A / BEvery program holds two complete sounds. They can be layered, split across the keyboard, or used one at a time.

Program menu

PROGCurrent program number, 0–99.
BANKProgram bank, 0 or 1.
WAVE / TRANWhich wavetable or transient the program loads, 0–127.
MIDITransmit and receive channel. 0 switches MIDI off.
DTFData transfer. Copies sounds between groups, 8 resets to factory, 9 stores. Returns to 0 after use.
SPLTSplit point for the split keyboard modes.
KEYBKeyboard mode: poly, quad, duo, unison or one of the splits.
TUNEMaster tuning in Hz, 400–499.
SYSEXManual data dump trigger, not a stored setting. Sits at 0 in normal use.
LOCLocal control. 0 disconnects the keyboard from the sound engine, for DAW use.

User menu: global, survives program changes

EXP-PEDWhat an expression pedal controls.
TOUCHWhether channel aftertouch is transmitted and received.
BRT / CNTLCD brightness and contrast.
VFirmware version.
PAR-COMWhether MIDI CC messages for sound parameters are sent, received, both or neither.
FIRMFirmware emulation: 0 original, 1 enhanced, 2 enhanced with exponential envelopes.
OSCOscillator quality: 0 original with stepping and aliasing, 1 morphed and cleaner.
LFOLFO precision: 0 original and wobbly, 1 modern and stable.

Digital menu

Most entries are two letters: source, then destination. Sources are K keyboard, M LFO or mod wheel, B pitch bend, T aftertouch, V velocity. Destinations are W wave position, F filter, L loudness, M modulation depth.

UWUpper Wavetable select: 0 normal, 1 upper table, 2 no wrapping. With a transient loaded it sets loop length instead.
SWSub oscillator mode, 0–6. Changes the sound more than any other parameter.
KW / KF / KLKeyboard to wave position, filter, loudness.
MW / MF / MLLFO and mod wheel to wave position, filter, loudness.
TW / TF / TLAftertouch to wave position, filter, loudness.
TMAftertouch controls the mod wheel.
VF / VLVelocity to filter, loudness.
BDBender destination: what the pitch wheel affects.
BIBender interval: its range, from two semitones to two octaves.

Tuning menu

DETUDetuning between sub and main oscillator. 1–4 are fine amounts, 5–9 fixed intervals.
MO / MSLFO pitch modulation of the main and sub oscillators, through the mod wheel.
EO / ESEnvelope 3 to main and sub oscillator pitch. EO values of 2–9 give portamento instead.
SEMITSemitone offset for each of the eight voices individually.

Sequencer menu

MODSequencer or one of the two arpeggiators, plus the arpeggio pattern.
CLKClock source and rate: internal, DIN MIDI, USB MIDI or analog trigger.
GATGate length as a percentage of the step, 1–99. Never long enough to tie steps together.
DIVClock division: which note value each step represents.
BPMTempo, 40–240.
MTNMetronome on or off.
OVPTwo digits: overdub on or off, and whether the keyboard transposes the sequence.
STEPCurrent step, then the total number recorded.
ppqnPulses per quarter note: the resolution of an external clock.

When something sounds wrong

SymptomCheck
No sound at allENV2 → Loudness is not at 00; LOC is 1 in the Program menu; Master Volume is up
Notes missing in the top octavesSEMIT values in the Tuning menu pushing them past the note range
Out of tuneDETU and SEMIT at 0; TUNE is 440; MO / MS / EO / ES are 0. If it persists, recalibrate: power on holding Analog + Group after a 45-minute warm-up. Takes up to 200 seconds.
A modulation route does nothingModulation sources sum. If something else has already hit the limit, more modulation has no effect. Zero the others and add back one at a time.
A transient barely playsENV1 → Waves is at or near 00, so the loop point sits at the start of the sample and only a few milliseconds repeat. Raise it until you hear the body of the recording.
Position 60 isn't a sawtoothUW is 2, which disables positions 60–63. They clamp to 59, so the top of the knob does nothing. Set UW 0 to reach the analog waveforms.
A sweep finishes early and holdsWaves Osc plus ENV1 → Waves exceeds the ceiling, so the position hits the top before the envelope does. Reduce the depth so the total lands at or below 59 at UW 2, or 63 at UW 0.
KF does nothingThe filter is probably wide open. Tracking raises the cutoff, so at VCF Cutoff 63 there is no headroom above it. Close the filter to 25 or so and try again.
Decay does nothingSustain is at maximum, so there is no level for the envelope to fall to. Lower Sustain and decay becomes audible.
Release does nothingThe envelope has already reached zero before you lift the key, usually because of Sustain 00 with a short decay. Raise Sustain, or release the key before the decay finishes.
Sweep clicks or jumps at the topUW is 0. Set it to 2.
Everything sounds wrong and you've lost trackDTF → 8 → 1. Resets to factory settings without touching user wavetables or transients, then rebuild the base patch.
LFO Delay does nothingMost likely you're on a fixed-depth route. In testing, delay reached the mod-wheel-scaled paths (MW 1, MF 1, MO/MS) but not MW 3–9, MF 4–9 or ML 1. See Lesson 05D for what was and wasn't tested.
An edit vanishedYou changed program, or powered off. Nothing is stored until DTF 9.
SynthTribe can't talk to the WaveThe manual says to set SYSEX in the Program menu to 1. SYSEX is a momentary dump trigger, not a mode you leave switched on, so during normal playing it sits at 0. Also try a shorter USB cable and skip any hub.

What to do after Lesson 11

Take a factory patch apart. Write down every value from the Digital, Tuning and Analog menus, then rebuild it from the base patch until they match. The 200 factory programs has both bank listings, a cross-reference from wavetable to the presets that use it, and six suggested starting points.

Parameter names, ranges and behaviours here come from the Behringer Wave user manual, V1.0 for firmware 1.0.11. Panel values are given on the Analog menu's 00–63 scale. Anything marked Verify is a detail the manual leaves open. Confirm it by ear before building a patch on it. Check your own firmware version at V in the User menu; envelope and oscillator behaviour has changed across updates.
