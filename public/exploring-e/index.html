The Cabinet of Curios

The Number e

Grow 1 by all of itself and you get 2. Grow it in smaller and smaller pieces, each building on the last, and you get a little more every time, but never more than 2.71828…. That number is e. Start on the number line, where e and its powers come from, then push sideways into the plane of complex numbers, where the same recipe walks around a circle.

Station 1

Growth on growth

Start at 1 and grow it by all of itself in one step: you land on 2. Now split that growth into n pieces. Each piece multiplies by 1 + 1/n, so it adds 1/n of whatever you have now, including what the earlier pieces added. That growth on growth lands you a little further every time you split it finer. Every landing leaves a mark. Zoom in as the marks bunch up: they crowd toward 2.71828…, but never get past it. That number is e.

Zoom

Station 2

Powers of e

The recipe takes any push x, not just 1: multiply by 1 + x/n, n times. Push by 2 and look halfway. The first half of the steps push by 1 in n/2 pieces, which is exactly Station 1's recipe, so they land near e. The second half do it again from there. So you land near e × e, and the recipe settles on ex, e raised to the power x. Pushes add while the results multiply.

Station 3

One sideways piece

Every push so far went forward, along the number line. Stand a second number line upright through 0 and the line becomes a plane, the plane of complex numbers, where multiplying by i turns a number a quarter turn. Now push sideways. Split a push of iθ into n pieces, and each piece multiplies by 1 + iθ/n. To see what one piece does, call wherever you are z. Multiplying it out, z(1 + iθ/n) = z + (θ/n)iz: you keep z and add a small piece of iz, which is z turned a quarter turn. So the piece is at right angles to the line from 0 to z, and it takes you to the amber point z′ = z(1 + iθ/n). Drag z anywhere and the piece turns with it. Slide Pieces taken to keep going: z′ becomes the new z, and the next piece is at right angles to that, so it leans further round. The dashed circle runs through where you started. A pure turn would keep you on it, but every piece also stretches you a little, so the walk drifts outward. Then shrink the pieces with the Piece size slider: a small sideways piece turns you by about its own size, in radians, and hardly stretches you at all.

Station 4

ei: Station 1, sideways

Station 1 started at 1 and multiplied by 1 + 1/n, n times, so each piece added 1/n of what you had. Change just one thing: make each piece i/n of what you have, and multiply by 1 + i/n instead. Each piece is now 1/n of where you are, turned a quarter turn, so it pushes sideways, and Station 3 showed that it turns you by about 1/n of a radian. All n pieces together turn you about 1 radian. It's Station 2's recipe with x = i, so the sideways walk settles on ei ≈ 0.5403 + 0.8415i, one radian round the circle. There's no π in it. Both walks below use the same n: the forward one is Station 1's, heading for e.

Station 5

Push sideways

Station 2 took Station 1's recipe and pushed by any x. Do the same with Station 4: push sideways by iθ, so each of the n pieces multiplies by 1 + iθ/n. Each piece turns you about θ/n radians, so together they walk a distance θ round the circle of radius 1, and as n grows the walk settles on eiθ. With a few pieces, a small square at each corner marks the right angle. Pushes add while results multiply, just as in Station 2. A push of 2i is two pushes of i in a row, so e2i = ei × ei, two radians round. Halfway round the circle is π radians, so a push of iπ lands on −1.

Station 6

Wrap the line around the circle

Each sideways piece is at right angles to the line from 0, so it slides along the circle instead of away from it. Pushing sideways by θ walks a distance θ around the circle. So here is eiθ without any pieces: take the number line from 0 to θ, stand it up at 1, and wrap it around the circle. Every number t on it lands on eit. The circle is 2π around, so π/2 reaches a quarter of the way, at i, and π reaches exactly halfway, at −1. Halfway is two quarter turns, so eiπ = i × i = −1, which is Euler's identity, eiπ + 1 = 0.

Station 7

Reading eiθ

Stations 5 and 6 found the same thing two ways. Put plainly, eiθ is where you end up after walking a distance θ round the circle from 1. Nothing gets added to 1. You only ever multiply, and each symbol has its own job. i sets the direction, θ sets how far, π comes from the circle, and e names where you land. Walk the circle and the readout shows each one at work.

Station 8

Both at once

Station 2 pushed forward and Station 5 pushed sideways. Push at a slant and you do both. Each piece multiplies by 1 + (a + bi)/n: the forward part a stretches and the sideways part b turns. You land on ea+bi, stretched to ea and turned b radians, so ea+bi = ea × ebi, a stretch times a turn. In between, the walk spirals.

Things to notice

  • e grows at the rate of its own size. Each piece adds 1/n of what you have now, so the bigger you get, the faster you grow. That's why e turns up wherever something grows or shrinks in proportion to how much there is: money earning interest, a population with room to spread, a cup of tea cooling toward room temperature.
  • The last piece adds about e times the first. In Station 1 the first of a million pieces adds a millionth of 1, and the last adds a millionth of nearly e. Growth on growth, all the way up.
  • Pushes add, results multiply. Pushing by 2 is pushing by 1 twice, so e2 = e × e. The same rule works sideways: turning by θ then by φ is turning by θ + φ, so eiθ × eiφ = ei(θ+φ). That's the complex numbers rule that angles add, written as powers.
  • eiθ isn't e multiplied by itself iθ times. That has no meaning. For a complex power, the recipe is what the power means, and it earns the name by following the rule above.
  • Why radians. Station 6 wraps the number line around a circle of radius 1, so a length along the line becomes the same length of arc. An angle measured by its arc on a circle of radius 1 is an angle in radians. In degrees the wrap would need stretching by π/180, which is why eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ only works with θ in radians.
  • Every number is a stretch and a turn. Any complex number can be written reiθ: a length r and an angle θ. Multiplying two of them multiplies the lengths and adds the angles in one step.
  • Five numbers in one line. eiπ + 1 = 0 uses 0, 1, e, i, and π once each, and nothing else. Read through Station 6, it says something plainer: walk halfway round a circle and you face the other way.

A little history

  • Found before anyone noticed it. In 1618 an appendix to an English edition of John Napier's work on logarithms gave a table of what we'd now call natural logarithms, logarithms to the base e. It carried no author's name, though it was probably by William Oughtred, and nobody picked out the base as a number worth naming.
  • A question about interest. Jacob Bernoulli worked on interest added more and more often: a year's interest of 100% added all at once, or in two halves, or monthly, or every instant. That's Station 1's recipe, (1 + 1/n)n. He published it in 1690 and showed that however often the interest is added, the total stays between 2 and 3.
  • Euler gave it its letter. Leonhard Euler wrote e for the number in a paper on firing cannons, written in 1727 or 1728 and not printed until 1862. He used it again in a letter to Christian Goldbach on 25 November 1731, and in print in his Mechanica of 1736. Nobody knows why he picked e. It almost certainly wasn't for his own name.
  • The recipe, in Euler's words. In his Introductio in analysin infinitorum of 1748, Euler wrote e as (1 + 1/i)i and ez as (1 + z/i)i, Stations 1 and 2 with an infinitely large number of pieces. A chapter later he put a sideways push through the same recipe, (1 + v√−1/i)i, and reached ev√−1 = cos v + √−1 sin v, which is Station 5. His i was that infinitely large number, not √−1. He only started writing i for √−1 in 1777.
  • Nobody knows who first wrote eiπ + 1 = 0. Roger Cotes had the formula in a disguised form, in the language of logarithms, in 1714, and Euler published it plainly in 1748. Either one gives Euler's identity in a line, but the historian Robin Wilson found that neither seems to have written it down, and that it's unknown who first did.
  • e never settles into a pattern of digits. Euler proved in 1737 that e isn't a fraction, so its decimals never repeat. In 1873 Charles Hermite went further and proved that no equation with whole-number coefficients has e as a solution, so e is transcendental. It was the first number shown to be transcendental that wasn't built for the purpose.
